\chapter{Data Structures}
\label{ch:datastructures}

The previous chapters computed with mathematics; this one steps back to ask what,
exactly, a value \emph{is} inside Mathilda. The answer is unusually simple, and it
is the foundation everything else rests on: there is only one kind of value, the
\emph{expression}, and every list, matrix, rule, pattern, and association you will
ever type is one. That uniformity is what lets a single handful of tools---\B{Part},
\B{Map}, \B{ReplaceAll}---work on everything without special cases. But simplicity at
the surface is not the same as naivety underneath. Behind the uniform tree, Mathilda
keeps specialized structures that make real work fast: the \emph{association},
a hash-backed key--value store with amortized \(O(1)\) lookup; the \emph{packed
array}, a dense machine-precision buffer that runs numeric code at the speed of C;
the \emph{sparse array}, which stores only the entries that are not zero; and the
\emph{linked list}, the one value that is changed in place.
The theme of the chapter is precisely this tension---one uniform data model on top,
invisible acceleration beneath---and how Mathilda keeps the two from ever
contradicting each other.

We build up in seven steps. First the expression tree itself: head and arguments,
atoms and compounds, and the tools that read a value's shape. Then lists---the
workhorse aggregate---their parts, spans, and the newer array operations. Then
associations, the key--value store. Then strings, the one atom with an internal
structure of its own, and the pattern language that takes them apart. Then
the packed-array substrate, where
most of the chapter's ``under the hood'' weight lies, because that is where a
transparent list quietly becomes a C buffer and back again. The last two sections
apply the same idea, an atom that behaves like a list, to sparse arrays and to the
mutable \texttt{LinkedList}.

\section{Everything is an expression}
\label{sec:ds-expressions}

Type \(a + b\,c\) and it looks like an infix formula. It is not stored as one.
Every compound value in Mathilda is a \emph{function application}\index{expression!head
and arguments}---a head applied to a sequence of arguments---and \B{FullForm} prints
that underlying tree with the surface syntax stripped away.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/expressions/1}{The sum-of-products \(a+bc\) is
\texttt{Plus} applied to \(a\) and \texttt{Times[b, c]}: operators are just
shorthand for named heads.}
\pair{06-data-structures/expressions/2}{A list is no different---\(\{1,2,3\}\) is
\texttt{List[1, 2, 3]}, the head \B{List} applied to three arguments.}
\pair{06-data-structures/expressions/3}{A fraction's full form is
\texttt{Rational[1, 2]}, which names its numerator and denominator. That is how it is
spelled, not what it is: as the next pairs show, \(1/2\) is a single number.}
\end{codepairs}

Because there is only one representation, the head of a value tells you what kind of
thing it is, and \B{Head} reads it off for anything at all---an operator expression,
an atom, a list, or a call to an undefined function.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/expressions/4}{The head of \(a+bc\) is \B{Plus}.}
\pair{06-data-structures/expressions/5}{The head of the sub-product \(bc\) is
\B{Times}.}
\pair{06-data-structures/expressions/6}{A machine integer has head \texttt{Integer}.}
\pair{06-data-structures/expressions/7}{A machine real has head \texttt{Real}.}
\pair{06-data-structures/expressions/8}{A fraction has head \B{Rational}.}
\pair{06-data-structures/expressions/9}{A complex number has head \B{Complex}.}
\pair{06-data-structures/expressions/10}{Strings carry head \texttt{String}.}
\pair{06-data-structures/expressions/11}{A list's head is \texttt{List}.}
\pair{06-data-structures/expressions/12}{An unknown \(f[x]\) has head
\(f\), the symbol you named. Nothing is privileged.}
\end{codepairs}

The values that have no internal parts to descend into---numbers, symbols,
strings---are the \emph{atoms}\index{atom}; everything else is a compound with a
head and arguments. \B{AtomQ} draws the line, and it is worth watching where it
falls, because ``looks compound'' and ``is compound'' are not the same.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/expressions/13}{An integer is an atom.}
\pair{06-data-structures/expressions/14}{So is a symbol.}
\pair{06-data-structures/expressions/15}{A \B{Rational} is one too, despite printing
as \(1/2\): it is indivisible as a value even though its
\texttt{FullForm} shows two integers.}
\pair{06-data-structures/expressions/16}{A function call is not an atom---you can
take it apart.}
\pair{06-data-structures/expressions/17}{Neither is a list.}
\end{codepairs}

\calloutnote{Under the hood}{An \texttt{Expr} is a tagged union
(\texttt{src/expr.h}): the tag is one of \texttt{EXPR\_INTEGER} (a machine
\texttt{int64}), \texttt{EXPR\_REAL} (a \texttt{double}), \texttt{EXPR\_SYMBOL},
\texttt{EXPR\_STRING}, \texttt{EXPR\_BIGINT} (a GMP integer), or
\texttt{EXPR\_FUNCTION} (a head pointer plus an argument array). The first five are
the atoms; only \texttt{EXPR\_FUNCTION} has parts. Expressions are treated as
immutable during evaluation---a transformation returns a new tree rather than
editing one in place---and \texttt{expr\_copy} is a reference-count bump, not a deep
clone, so this costs far less than it sounds. A \texttt{Rational} and a
\texttt{Complex} are \texttt{EXPR\_FUNCTION} nodes at the level of storage, but they
are numbers, and every structural builtin treats them as atoms. One helper,
\texttt{expr\_is\_number\_atom} (\texttt{src/common.h}), makes the decision for
\B{AtomQ}, \B{Length}, \B{Dimensions}, \B{Apply}, \texttt{ReplacePart}, \B{Take},
\B{Append}, \B{Sort}, and the rest, so they cannot disagree. Before v0.6535 they did:
\texttt{Length[3/4]} was \(2\) and \texttt{Append[3/4, 1]} built the malformed
\texttt{Rational[3, 4, 1]}.}

\index{atom!Rational and Complex}The structural builtins agree with \B{AtomQ}. The
pattern matcher alone still sees the two integers inside, which is what lets a rule
take a fraction apart into numerator and denominator.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/expressions/22}{A fraction and a complex number have no
parts to count: \B{Length} is \(0\) and \B{Dimensions} is \(\{\}\), as for any
other number.}
\pair{06-data-structures/expressions/23}{\B{Apply} has no head to replace, so the
number comes back unchanged.}
\pair{06-data-structures/expressions/24}{Patterns still match the stored form:
\texttt{Rational[a\_, b\_]} matches \(1/2\), binding \(a=1\) and \(b=2\).}
\end{codepairs}

The shape of a tree---how deeply it nests, and what lives at each level---is itself
queryable. \bidx{Depth}\mcode{Depth} gives one more than the longest path from the
root to a leaf, and \B{Level} collects the subexpressions at a chosen depth.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/expressions/18}{\mcode{Depth} of \(f[g[h[x]]]\) is
\(4\): three nested heads plus the atom \(x\) at the bottom.}
\pair{06-data-structures/expressions/19}{A \(2\times2\) matrix has depth \(3\)---the
outer list, the rows, and the numbers.}
\pair{06-data-structures/expressions/20}{\B{Level} with the specification
\(\{-1\}\) gathers the atoms (level \(-1\) counts up from the leaves): every
number and symbol at the bottom of \(a + f[x, y^n]\).}
\pair{06-data-structures/expressions/21}{Level \(2\) instead gathers everything
within two steps of the root, so \(y^n\) appears whole rather than split into
\(y\) and \(n\).}
\end{codepairs}

A negative level counts distance from the leaves and a positive one distance from
the root, so \(\{-1\}\) is ``the atoms'' and \(2\) is ``down to two levels
deep.''\index{levelspec} These level specifications are shared by \B{Map},
\B{Cases}, \B{Position}, and the rest of the structural operators---learn them once
and they apply everywhere, which is the recurring dividend of the uniform model.

\section{Lists}
\label{sec:ds-lists}

The list is the aggregate you will reach for most, and because it is just an
\texttt{EXPR\_FUNCTION} with head \texttt{List}, every generic tool applies to it
unchanged. What lists add is a rich vocabulary for addressing their elements.

\subsection{Parts and spans}
\label{ssec:ds-parts}

\B{Part}---written \(\mathit{expr}\)\texttt{[[\ldots]]}---extracts an element by
position, counting from \(1\), or from the end with negative indices. A
\emph{span}\index{list!span}---the \B{Span} operator, written \texttt{i ;; j}---extracts
a contiguous run, optionally with a step; and for a nested list a comma-separated
index descends level by level.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/lists/2}{The third element of the vector of primes.}
\pair{06-data-structures/lists/3}{A negative index counts from the end: \(-1\) is
the last.}
\pair{06-data-structures/lists/4}{A span \texttt{2 ;; 4} takes elements two through
four inclusive.}
\pair{06-data-structures/lists/5}{With a step, \texttt{1 ;; -1 ;; 2} takes every
second element from first to last.}
\pair{06-data-structures/lists/7}{On a matrix, \texttt{[[2, 3]]} descends: row
two, then column three.}
\pair{06-data-structures/lists/8}{\texttt{All} in a slot keeps that whole axis, so
\texttt{[[All, 2]]} is the second column.}
\end{codepairs}

The same \texttt{[[\ldots]]} notation reads a single element, a slice, or a whole
axis depending on what you put in the slot, and it composes down through any depth
of nesting---a direct consequence of a matrix being nothing more special than a list
of lists.

\subsection{Shape and nesting}
\label{ssec:ds-shape}

\B{Length} counts the top-level arguments of any expression, and \B{Dimensions}
reports the rectangular shape of a nested list---the lengths at each level, read
outward in.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/lists/9}{Six primes, length six.}
\pair{06-data-structures/lists/10}{\B{Length} of the matrix is \(2\)---it has two
rows; length is always the count of immediate arguments.}
\pair{06-data-structures/lists/11}{\B{Dimensions} gives the full shape:
\(2\times3\).}
\pair{06-data-structures/lists/12}{And for a rank-\(3\) array, all three axis
lengths.}
\pair{06-data-structures/lists/13}{Because \B{Length} is generic, it counts the
arguments of any head---here the four arguments of \(f\).}
\end{codepairs}

The contrast between the last two pairs is the point: \B{Dimensions} presumes the
rectangular, tensor-like structure a numeric array has, while \B{Length} makes no
such assumption and works on \(f[a,b,c,d]\) exactly as on a list. One is for data;
both are for expressions.

\subsection{Reshaping, padding, and gradients}
\label{ssec:ds-arrayops}

Four operations turn a flat list into a shaped one, resize it, differentiate it, and
locate a value inside it. \B{ArrayReshape} pours the elements of a list, in
row-major order, into an array of whatever shape you request; \B{ArrayPad} grows (or,
with negative amounts, trims) an array at its edges under a choice of fill scheme;
\B{ListGradient} takes the numerical derivative of sampled data by finite
differences; and \B{FirstPosition} finds where a pattern first matches.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/arrayops/1}{\B{ArrayReshape} lays \(1\ldots12\) into a
\(3\times4\) grid, filling row by row.}
\pair{06-data-structures/arrayops/2}{When the data runs short, the third argument
supplies the fill: seven elements into a \(3\times3\) grid, padded with \(x\).}
\pair{06-data-structures/arrayops/3}{\B{ArrayPad} adds two elements of the default
fill (\(0\)) at each end.}
\pair{06-data-structures/arrayops/4}{An asymmetric \(\{1,2\}\) pad with the
\texttt{"Fixed"} scheme repeats the edge values rather than inserting zeros.}
\pair{06-data-structures/arrayops/5}{A negative amount trims: \(-2\) drops two from
each end.}
\end{codepairs}

\B{ListGradient} is the most mathematically interesting of the four---a
from-first-principles port of \texttt{numpy.gradient}. On the interior of the array
it takes a second-order central difference; at each endpoint, where a centered
stencil would run off the edge, it falls back to a one-sided difference.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/arrayops/6}{Sample \(x^2\) at \(x=1,\dots,5\)
(\(\{1,4,9,16,25\}\)); the interior values \(4,6,8\) are exactly the derivative
\(2x\) at \(x=2,3,4\), and the ends \(3,9\) are the one-sided approximations.}
\pair{06-data-structures/arrayops/7}{With symbolic input the finite-difference
formula itself is returned---halved central differences inside, full one-sided
differences at the ends.}
\pair{06-data-structures/arrayops/8}{Sampling \(x^3\) and asking for a fourth-order
stencil recovers \(3x^2\) exactly at every point, endpoints included.}
\end{codepairs}

\calloutnote{Theory}{The single kernel behind \B{ListGradient} is Fornberg's
finite-difference-weight recurrence\index{finite differences!Fornberg weights},
which produces the exact weights for a derivative of any order on any (even
non-uniform) grid. A second-order interior stencil is
\((f_{i+1}-f_{i-1})/2h\)---which is why sampling a quadratic gives the derivative
back with no error, since the central difference of \(x^2\) is exact---and the
endpoints drop to first order (numpy's \texttt{edge\_order = 1}). Because the weights
are exact rationals, integer input yields exact \B{Rational} output and symbolic
input a symbolic formula, as the middle pair shows; only a machine-real array takes
the packed floating-point fast path of \S\ref{sec:ds-packed}.}

\B{FirstPosition} reports the position of the first match as a list of indices---the
very indices \B{Part} would use to extract it---searching depth-first through the
whole expression.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/arrayops/9}{The value \(7\) sits at position \(\{3\}\).}
\pair{06-data-structures/arrayops/10}{In a nested list the position is a full index
path: \(4\) is at row two, column two.}
\pair{06-data-structures/arrayops/11}{No match returns \(\mcode{Missing["NotFound"]}\).}
\pair{06-data-structures/arrayops/12}{If you supply a default, it is
returned in its place.}
\end{codepairs}

\calloutnote{Pitfall}{\B{FirstPosition} takes its arguments with the attribute
\texttt{HoldRest}, so the default is evaluated only if it is actually
returned---a deliberate design that lets you write an expensive fallback without
paying for it on a hit. The search follows \B{Position}'s conventions exactly
(levels \(\{0,\infty\}\), \texttt{Heads -> True}), which is why a position can be
the empty list \(\{\}\), meaning the whole expression matched.}

\section{Associations}
\label{sec:ds-associations}

A list indexes by position. Often what you have instead is a set of named
fields---a record, a lookup table, a histogram---and forcing that onto positional
storage means remembering that ``price is element three.'' The \B{Association}\index{association!keyed
store}, written \texttt{<|key -> value, \ldots|>}, indexes by \emph{key}: any expression can
be a key, keys are unique, and they keep their insertion order.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/associations/1}{An inventory keyed by fruit name. It prints
back in the order written.}
\pair{06-data-structures/associations/2}{Applied like a function,
\(\mathit{assoc}[\mathit{key}]\) looks the key up---the idiomatic accessor.}
\pair{06-data-structures/associations/3}{The \texttt{[[key]]} form does the same
through \B{Part}.}
\pair{06-data-structures/associations/4}{\B{Keys} lists the keys, in order.}
\pair{06-data-structures/associations/5}{\B{Values} lists the corresponding
values.}
\pair{06-data-structures/associations/6}{\B{Lookup} is the explicit accessor.}
\pair{06-data-structures/associations/7}{Its three-argument form supplies a default
for an absent key---the safe way to read data that may be incomplete.}
\pair{06-data-structures/associations/8}{\B{KeyExistsQ} tests for a key's
presence.}
\pair{06-data-structures/associations/9}{It reports \texttt{False} when the key is
missing.}
\pair{06-data-structures/associations/10}{A bare lookup of an absent key returns a
\mcode{Missing} marker naming what was not found, rather than an error.}
\end{codepairs}

Two behaviors set associations apart from a bag of rules. Keys are unique with
last-value-wins, and functions that consume a collection act on the
\emph{values}\index{association!value threading} while keeping keys aligned.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/associations/11}{A repeated key collapses: the later value
wins, but the key keeps its first position.}
\pair{06-data-structures/associations/12}{\B{Map} threads over values and returns an
association---keys ride along untouched.}
\pair{06-data-structures/associations/13}{\B{Total} reduces over the values, exactly
as it would over \texttt{Values[inv]}.}
\pair{06-data-structures/associations/14}{\B{AssociationThread} zips a list of keys
against a list of values into an association---the constructor to reach for when the
two arrive separately.}
\end{codepairs}

\calloutnote{Under the hood}{Single-key access is amortized \(O(1)\). A persistent
key\(\rightarrow\)position hash index (\texttt{src/assoc\_index.h}) is cached on the
association and built on the first single-key read, so \B{Lookup}, \B{KeyExistsQ},
\(\mathit{a}[\mathit{key}]\), and \(\mathit{a}[[\mathit{key}]]\) never scan the
entries. The index reuses spare space already on the expression node, so it costs no
extra memory, and it is transparent to \B{SameQ}, \B{Sort}, and \B{Union}, which see
the association exactly as before. The value-threading rule---that \B{Map}, \B{Total},
\B{Select}, \B{Cases}, and their kin operate on values and keep keys aligned---runs
through one shared helper, which is why these operations compose predictably into
data pipelines.}

\mcode{Part} addresses an association by key exactly as it addresses a list by position,
and a part that selects \emph{several} entries returns a
\emph{sub-association}\index{association!slicing}---the
chosen entries, keys and all, in their original order.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/assoc-part/1}{A small keyed record to slice.}
\pair{06-data-structures/assoc-part/2}{A span selects a contiguous run of entries and
keeps them keyed.}
\pair{06-data-structures/assoc-part/3}{A list of keys picks exactly those, in the order
requested.}
\pair{06-data-structures/assoc-part/4}{Integer positions return the same
sub-association---position and key are two names for one slot.}
\end{codepairs}

\subsection{Associations are atoms}
\label{ssec:ds-assoc-atoms}

An association looks like a container, but to the structural machinery it is a single,
indivisible object---an \emph{atom}\index{association!atomicity}\index{atom!association}.
That is the rule that lets one travel safely through
code that knows nothing about associations. Traversal and testing see the \emph{values};
the keys are addressing metadata, reached only through an explicit \B{Key}.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/assoc-atoms/1}{A three-entry association.}
\pair{06-data-structures/assoc-atoms/2}{\B{AtomQ} reports \texttt{True}: structurally it
is one node, not a compound of parts to be taken apart.}
\pair{06-data-structures/assoc-atoms/3}{\mcode{Cases} scans the \emph{values}, so a
pattern filter returns matching values, not entries.}
\pair{06-data-structures/assoc-atoms/4}{\mcode{Position} likewise finds a value, and
names where it lives by its key, wrapped in \B{Key}.}
\pair{06-data-structures/assoc-atoms/5}{And \B{ReplaceAll} cannot touch a key: a rule
that would rewrite \texttt{"y"} leaves the association unchanged, because the keys are not
part of the expression it walks.}
\end{codepairs}

\calloutnote{Under the hood}{Atomicity is a deliberate---and recent---design choice.
\mcode{AtomQ}, \mcode{Depth}, \mcode{LeafCount}, \mcode{Level}, \mcode{Cases},
\mcode{Count}, \mcode{MemberQ}, \mcode{FreeQ}, \mcode{Position}, and the level
specifications of \mcode{Map} and \mcode{Apply} all agree to see only an association's
values. The pay-off is safety: because \texttt{/.} can never merge or rewrite keys, and a
pattern scan can never mistake a key for a value, an association carried through a generic
pipeline---as a rule set, a \mcode{Cases} filter, or a user-defined transformation---behaves
predictably and cannot be silently corrupted. When you \emph{do} need to name a key
structurally, \mcode{Position} hands back a \B{Key}$[\,k\,]$ wrapper that \mcode{Part}
accepts directly.}

\subsection{Operator forms, slots, and pipelines}
\label{ssec:ds-assoc-pipeline}

Data work is a pipeline: filter the rows, sort them, pull a field, summarise. Associations
are built for it, through two conveniences. A pure function can read a field by \emph{name}
with a named slot\index{slot!named}, \texttt{\#field}, and most operators carry a
\emph{curried} form\index{operator form}\index{association!operator form} that
takes their data last, so stages compose left to right.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/assoc-pipeline/2}{\B{Select} keeps the rows whose \texttt{qty}
field---read by the named slot \texttt{\#qty}---exceeds two.}
\pair{06-data-structures/assoc-pipeline/3}{\B{SortBy} orders the same rows by that field.}
\pair{06-data-structures/assoc-pipeline/5}{\mcode{Lookup["pears"]} is an operator form:
it waits for an association, then looks the key up.}
\pair{06-data-structures/assoc-pipeline/6}{Listable heads thread over the values, so
adding a scalar adds it to each value and keeps the keys aligned.}
\pair{06-data-structures/assoc-pipeline/7}{And \mcode{Total} of a \emph{list} of
associations sums them key by key---the reduction a table of records calls for.}
\end{codepairs}

\calloutnote{Under the hood}{A named slot \texttt{\#field} parses as
\B{Slot}\texttt{["field"]}, which on an association reads that key---so
\texttt{\#qty\,>\,2\,\&} is an ordinary pure function that happens to index by name. The
curried forms come from one table-driven mechanism (\texttt{src/opform.c}) that gives some
forty-three heads---\mcode{Lookup}, \mcode{Select}, \mcode{Map}, \mcode{GroupBy},
\mcode{SortBy}, and their kin---an operator form \texttt{f[args][data]} equivalent to
\texttt{f[data, args]}. Together they let a data pipeline be written point-free, each stage
a self-contained operator applied in turn.}

\subsection{Aggregating: Counts, GroupBy, and Merge}
\label{ssec:ds-aggregate}

The reason associations matter for data work is that they are what the aggregation
operators \emph{produce}. \B{Counts} tallies a list into an association from value to
frequency; \B{GroupBy} partitions a list by a key function; and \B{Merge} combines
several associations, reconciling clashing keys with a function of your choosing.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/aggregate/1}{\B{Counts} turns a list of observations into
an empirical frequency table---the discrete distribution of the sample.}
\pair{06-data-structures/aggregate/2}{\B{GroupBy} partitions \(1\ldots10\) by
\texttt{EvenQ}: two groups, keyed \texttt{False} and \texttt{True}.}
\pair{06-data-structures/aggregate/3}{Its three-argument form applies a reducer to
each group---here \B{Total}, collapsing the split-apply-combine in one call.}
\pair{06-data-structures/aggregate/5}{With a \(\mathit{key}\to\mathit{value}\) rule,
\B{GroupBy} groups the rows by their \B{First} field but collects the \B{Last}: sales
gathered per category.}
\pair{06-data-structures/aggregate/6}{Add a reducer and the same call groups,
extracts, and totals---a one-line group-and-sum.}
\pair{06-data-structures/aggregate/7}{\B{Merge} unions the keys of two associations
and applies \B{Total} to the values collected under each: the shared key \(y\) sums
its two contributions, the unshared keys pass through.}
\end{codepairs}

The middle pairs are the split-apply-combine idiom\index{split-apply-combine} that
data analysis lives on: \emph{split} the data into groups, \emph{apply} a summary to
each, \emph{combine} the summaries into one table. \B{GroupBy} does all three, and
because the result is an association it feeds straight into another aggregation---the
frequency table from \B{Counts} is exactly the empirical distribution the statistics
chapter (\S\ref{sec:statistics}) builds its estimators on.

\B{Merge} is one of a family that treats an association as a keyed
\emph{set}\index{association!key-set algebra}. The keys
can be intersected, subtracted, and unioned; entries can be dropped by a predicate on
their values; and two tables can be joined on a shared field the way a database would.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/assoc-keyalgebra/1}{\B{KeyIntersection} restricts each
association to the keys they all share---here only \texttt{"b"}, each keeping its own
value.}
\pair{06-data-structures/assoc-keyalgebra/2}{\B{KeyComplement} keeps the first
association's keys that appear in no other.}
\pair{06-data-structures/assoc-keyalgebra/3}{\B{KeyUnion} aligns several associations to
their combined key set, filling a gap with a \mcode{Missing} marker.}
\pair{06-data-structures/assoc-keyalgebra/4}{\B{Discard} is \mcode{Select}'s complement:
it drops the entries whose value satisfies the test.}
\pair{06-data-structures/assoc-keyalgebra/5}{\B{JoinAcross} joins two lists of records on
a shared key, merging each matched pair into one row---a relational join.}
\pair{06-data-structures/assoc-keyalgebra/6}{\B{PositionIndex} inverts a list into an
association from each value to the positions it occupies.}
\pair{06-data-structures/assoc-keyalgebra/7}{And \mcode{Merge} accepts a plain list of
rules, collecting clashing keys before it reduces---so it doubles as a grouping tally.}
\end{codepairs}

\section{Strings and string patterns}
\label{sec:ds-strings}

Text is data too: a configuration file, a log line, the words of a document. A
\emph{string}\index{string} in Mathilda is an atom---head \texttt{String}, no parts
for \mcode{Part} to descend into---and that is the right model for the evaluator,
which treats \texttt{"Hello"} as a single value exactly as it treats \(42\). But a
string plainly \emph{has} internal structure, a sequence of characters, and a whole
family of functions reaches inside it. This section covers the everyday operations,
then the pattern language that generalizes them, and closes by turning a page of raw
text into an association.

\subsection{The basic operations}
\label{ssec:ds-string-basics}

\B{StringLength} counts characters; \B{StringTake} cuts a piece out by position,
with the same positive-from-the-front, negative-from-the-back conventions as
\mcode{Part}; \B{StringJoin}, written \texttt{<>}, concatenates; and
\B{StringSplit} breaks a string into a list of pieces.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/strings/1}{A string to work with.}
\pair{06-data-structures/strings/2}{It is an atom, its head is \texttt{String}, and
\mcode{StringLength} counts its twelve characters, the comma and space included.}
\pair{06-data-structures/strings/3}{\mcode{StringTake} with \(5\) takes the first five
characters.}
\pair{06-data-structures/strings/4}{With \(-5\), the last five.}
\pair{06-data-structures/strings/5}{\texttt{<>} is \mcode{StringJoin}: plain
concatenation, any number of pieces.}
\pair{06-data-structures/strings/6}{With one argument \mcode{StringSplit} splits at runs
of whitespace, so the double space yields no empty piece.}
\pair{06-data-structures/strings/7}{With an explicit delimiter every occurrence
counts, and the empty field between the two commas is kept.}
\end{codepairs}

The editing operations each return a new string---a string, being an atom, is never
modified in place. \B{StringReplace} rewrites by rules, \B{ToUpperCase} maps case,
\B{Characters} explodes a string into a list of one-character strings, and
\B{StringRiffle} goes the other way, joining a list with a separator.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/strings/8}{\mcode{StringReplace} takes a rule (or a list of
them) and rewrites every match.}
\pair{06-data-structures/strings/9}{\mcode{ToUpperCase} maps every letter; the
punctuation passes through.}
\pair{06-data-structures/strings/10}{\mcode{Characters} gives the list view of a string,
the bridge to every list operation of \S\ref{sec:ds-lists}.}
\pair{06-data-structures/strings/11}{\B{StringReverse} reverses by character.}
\pair{06-data-structures/strings/12}{\mcode{StringRiffle} interleaves a separator
between the elements of a list and joins the result.}
\end{codepairs}

Strings also have a canonical \emph{order}\index{string!canonical order}, which
\B{Sort}, \B{Union}, \B{Order}, and every other ordered result use. It is not byte
order. Strings are compared first on their letters and digits alone, with punctuation
ignored and case folded; ties are then broken by where the punctuation falls, and
last by punctuation rank or letter case, lower case first.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/strings/16}{\texttt{"a-b"} sorts beside \texttt{"ab"}
because the hyphen is ignored at first, \texttt{"a.m"} after them because \(m\) follows
\(b\), and \texttt{".hid"} last because it is compared as \texttt{hid}.}
\end{codepairs}

The last pair of tools crosses the boundary between text and expressions.
\B{ToString} renders any expression as the string you would see printed, and
\B{ToExpression} parses a string back into an expression \emph{and evaluates
it}\index{string!round trip with expressions}---so a string can carry a whole
computation.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/strings/13}{\mcode{ToString} gives the printed form, as a
string: the canonical order \(1 + x^2\), not the order typed.}
\pair{06-data-structures/strings/14}{\mcode{ToExpression} parses it back into the
polynomial.}
\pair{06-data-structures/strings/15}{Parsing is followed by evaluation, so a string
holding a \mcode{Sum} comes back as \(5050\).}
\end{codepairs}

\calloutnote{Pitfall}{\mcode{ToString} uses \texttt{OutputForm} by default, the same
form the REPL prints, so a string inside the expression loses its quotation marks:
\mcode{ToString[\{"a"\}]} is the three-character string \texttt{\{a\}}. For a string
that \mcode{ToExpression} can read back exactly, ask for
\mcode{ToString[expr, InputForm]}. And because \mcode{ToExpression} evaluates what it
parses, it should never be fed text from a source you do not trust.}

\subsection{String patterns}
\label{ssec:ds-string-patterns}

A literal search string is the simplest pattern, but most text-processing questions
are about \emph{kinds} of text: a run of digits, a word, whatever lies between two
brackets. Mathilda answers them with \emph{string patterns}\index{string!patterns},
built from the same pieces as the expression patterns of
\S\ref{sec:prog-patterns}. A \B{StringExpression}, written \texttt{\textasciitilde\textasciitilde},
concatenates pieces; character classes such as \mcode{DigitCharacter},
\mcode{LetterCharacter}, \mcode{WordCharacter}, and \mcode{Whitespace} stand for
families of characters; \texttt{p..} repeats; and the blanks \texttt{\_},
\texttt{\_\_}, \texttt{\_\_\_} match one, one-or-more, and zero-or-more characters.
\B{StringCases} extracts every match, and \B{StringMatchQ} asks whether the whole
string matches.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/string-patterns/1}{\texttt{DigitCharacter..} is a run of
one or more digits; \mcode{StringCases} returns each maximal run.}
\pair{06-data-structures/string-patterns/2}{\mcode{Whitespace} is any run of
spaces, so this collapses irregular spacing to single blanks.}
\pair{06-data-structures/string-patterns/3}{\mcode{StringMatchQ} tests the
\emph{whole} string: all letters, so \texttt{True}.}
\end{codepairs}

Naming a piece---\texttt{x\_\_}, or \texttt{x:\,p} for any pattern
\(p\)---captures the text it matched, and a delayed rule \texttt{:>} can use that name
on its right-hand side. This is where string patterns earn their keep: they do not
just find text, they take it apart\index{string!named captures}.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/string-patterns/4}{\texttt{x\_\_} binds whatever precedes
\texttt{"123"}, and the rule returns just that piece.}
\pair{06-data-structures/string-patterns/5}{Three named digit runs, reassembled in a
new order: an ISO date rewritten day-first.}
\pair{06-data-structures/string-patterns/6}{A blank sequence is \emph{greedy}: it
runs to the \emph{last} \texttt{">"} it can reach, swallowing both tags.}
\pair{06-data-structures/string-patterns/7}{\B{Shortest} makes it lazy, so each
match stops at the first closing bracket. (\B{Longest} names the greedy
default.)}
\pair{06-data-structures/string-patterns/8}{A name used twice must match the
\emph{same} text both times: \texttt{x\_ \textasciitilde\textasciitilde\ x\_} finds
doubled letters.}
\pair{06-data-structures/string-patterns/11}{A condition \texttt{/;} filters the
matches to runs of digits that are prime, but it also accepts the \(2\) inside
\(42\), which was not one of the numbers.}
\pair{06-data-structures/string-patterns/12}{Anchoring each run between
\mcode{WordBoundary}s makes the condition see whole numbers only, so the stray
\(2\) is gone.}
\end{codepairs}

The \(2\) in the first of those last two pairs is not a bug; it is backtracking doing
its job. When \(42\) fails the primality test, the engine is entitled to try a
shorter run starting one character later, and \texttt{"2"} passes. A condition
constrains which matches are accepted, not where they may start---so if the
\emph{units} of a match matter, say so in the pattern itself.

When the pattern vocabulary runs out, \B{RegularExpression} drops into raw
regular-expression syntax, and only there do \texttt{"\$1"}-style group references
work on the right-hand side. A rule whose right-hand side is not a string makes
\mcode{StringReplace} return a \mcode{StringExpression}---the string with a symbolic
hole in it.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/string-patterns/9}{\mcode{RegularExpression} with a capture
group; \texttt{"\$1"} names the group's text.}
\pair{06-data-structures/string-patterns/10}{Replacing \texttt{"b"} by the symbol
\(z\) cannot give a string, so the result is a \mcode{StringExpression}, printed
with \texttt{\textasciitilde\textasciitilde}.}
\pair{06-data-structures/string-patterns/13}{Group references work anywhere in the
right-hand side, here inside a list: each match becomes a name--value pair.}
\end{codepairs}

\usagebox{StringCases}

\calloutnote{Under the hood}{There is no separate string-matching engine in
Mathilda. One translator (\texttt{src/strings/regex/string\_pattern.c}) compiles a
string pattern into the source of a PCRE2 regular expression, and every pattern
function---\mcode{StringCases}, \mcode{StringReplace}, \mcode{StringSplit},
\mcode{StringMatchQ}, \mcode{StringPosition}, \mcode{StringCount},
\mcode{StringContainsQ}, and the rest---shares it, so a construct added there
becomes available to all of them at once. The mapping is direct:
\texttt{\textasciitilde\textasciitilde} is concatenation, \texttt{|} an alternation,
\texttt{p..} becomes \texttt{(?:p)+}, \texttt{\_\_} becomes \texttt{.+},
\mcode{Shortest} switches the quantifiers to their lazy forms, and a named piece
\texttt{x:\,p} becomes a capture group. A name's \emph{second} occurrence is emitted
as a backreference to that group, which is exactly why \texttt{x\_
\textasciitilde\textasciitilde\ x\_} means ``the same character twice.'' The
translator keeps a table from group numbers to names, and after a match the captured
texts are bound to those names and the rule's right-hand side is evaluated with them
in scope---the step that makes \texttt{:> x} return the piece rather than the whole
match. A \texttt{/;} condition or a \texttt{?test} cannot be expressed as a regular
expression at all: it needs Mathilda to evaluate code in the middle of a match. PCRE2
\emph{callouts} provide exactly that hook, and a callout that answers ``no'' makes the
regex engine backtrack and try the next alternative---the behavior you saw with the
primes. Compiling a pattern (translation, \texttt{pcre2\_compile}, then a JIT pass) is
the expensive step, so compiled rule sets are kept in a 64-entry least-recently-used
cache keyed by the pattern's structural hash. A pattern applied to forty thousand
separate strings is compiled once. A pattern the translator does not understand
leaves the call unevaluated rather than guessing.}

\calloutnote{Performance}{The cache is what makes the common idiom of mapping one
pattern over many strings cheap. On this build, forty thousand separate
\mcode{StringMatchQ} calls with the same pattern take about \(40\) milliseconds---one
microsecond each, nearly all of it evaluator overhead rather than matching---and the
listable form \mcode{StringMatchQ[list, p]}, which skips that overhead, about
\(7\). The commit that introduced the cache recorded the mapped form at \(556\)
milliseconds beforehand.}

\subsection{Characters, not bytes}
\label{ssec:ds-string-unicode}

Mathilda stores a string as UTF-8, in which a Greek letter occupies two bytes and
many other characters three or four. Every string function nonetheless counts and
indexes \emph{characters}\index{string!Unicode characters}---Unicode code points---so
the byte encoding never shows. Named characters are typed with the
\texttt{\textbackslash[\emph{Name}]} escape\index{named character}, and a code point
by number with \texttt{\textbackslash:\emph{hhhh}}.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/string-unicode/1}{The string \(\alpha\beta\gamma\), typed
with named-character escapes.}
\pair{06-data-structures/string-unicode/2}{Three characters---although the string
occupies six bytes.}
\pair{06-data-structures/string-unicode/3}{\B{ToCharacterCode} gives the code points,
U+03B1 to U+03B3.}
\pair{06-data-structures/string-unicode/4}{Indexing is by character too: the second
character is \(\beta\), not the second byte of \(\alpha\).}
\pair{06-data-structures/string-unicode/5}{Case mapping is full Unicode:
\(\alpha\beta\gamma\) becomes \(\mathrm{A}\mathrm{B}\Gamma\), code points 913--915.}
\pair{06-data-structures/string-unicode/6}{The two escapes and
\B{FromCharacterCode} all denote the same one-character string.}
\pair{06-data-structures/string-unicode/7}{An accented letter is one character:
\texttt{caf\'e} has length four.}
\pair{06-data-structures/string-unicode/8}{And the pattern classes are Unicode-aware:
Greek letters are \mcode{LetterCharacter}s.}
\pair{06-data-structures/string-unicode/9}{Asking \mcode{ToCharacterCode} for the
\texttt{"UTF8"} encoding shows the six bytes underneath, two per Greek letter.}
\end{codepairs}

\calloutnote{Under the hood}{The character view lives in
\texttt{src/strings/utf8.c}. A string that is pure ASCII---detected a machine word at
a time---is indexed by plain byte arithmetic, so the common case costs nothing extra;
otherwise the helpers walk the UTF-8 sequences to translate a character position into
a byte offset. A byte that does not begin a valid sequence counts as one character on
its own, so malformed input is never split mid-character and round-trips unchanged.
The regular-expression side is put in the same frame by compiling every pattern in
PCRE2's UTF and Unicode-property modes, which is what makes \mcode{LetterCharacter}
match \(\alpha\) and \texttt{IgnoreCase -> True} fold Greek as well as Latin.}

The same escapes work outside strings. Since v0.587 the parser reads every
non-ASCII character in source code, whether escaped (\texttt{\textbackslash[Mu]},
\texttt{\textbackslash:03bc}) or typed raw (\(\mu\) as UTF-8), the way the reference
parser does\index{named character!in source code}. A letter forms part of an
identifier, so \texttt{\textbackslash[Alpha]} and a raw \(\alpha\) are the same
symbol. A constant letter on its own is the constant (\texttt{\textbackslash[Pi]} is
\B{Pi}). An operator character such as \texttt{\textbackslash[Rule]} or
\texttt{\textbackslash[Element]} has the head, precedence and grouping the reference
gives it. Since v0.6001 the parser also reads the \emph{linear box syntax}
\texttt{\textbackslash!\textbackslash(\ldots\textbackslash)}\index{linear box
syntax}, the form a notebook uses when typeset input is copied out as text. The boxes
are read as the expressions they display.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/string-unicode/10}{\texttt{\textbackslash[Alpha]} in source
is an ordinary symbol, so a rule can replace it.}
\pair{06-data-structures/string-unicode/11}{Its name is the Greek letter itself:
\B{SymbolName} gives the one-character string \(\mu\), code point 956.}
\pair{06-data-structures/string-unicode/12}{\texttt{\textbackslash[Pi]} is the
constant \B{Pi}, \texttt{\textbackslash[Rule]} is \texttt{->}, and
\texttt{\textbackslash[Element]} is the infix form of \B{Element}.}
\pair{06-data-structures/string-unicode/13}{A \texttt{FractionBox} and a
\texttt{SqrtBox} in linear syntax read as \(\tfrac12\) and \(\sqrt{16}\), and they
evaluate.}
\pair{06-data-structures/string-unicode/14}{A \texttt{SubscriptBox} reads as
\texttt{Subscript}.}
\end{codepairs}

The explicit \texttt{\textbackslash*Box[\ldots]} forms are what a notebook writes, but
linear syntax also has short infix operators, and you are more likely to type those
by hand.\index{linear box syntax!infix operators} Inside
\texttt{\textbackslash!\textbackslash(\ldots\textbackslash)},
\texttt{\textbackslash\^{}} makes a superscript and \texttt{\textbackslash\_} a
subscript, \texttt{\textbackslash/} a fraction and \texttt{\textbackslash@} a square
root; \texttt{\textbackslash\&} and \texttt{\textbackslash+} put one thing over or
under another, and \texttt{\textbackslash\%} adds the second script of a sub- and
superscript pair. Each reads as the box it abbreviates, so it means what the
explicit box would.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/string-unicode/15}{A superscript is a power, and a subscript
is \texttt{Subscript}.}
\pair{06-data-structures/string-unicode/16}{The fraction and the square root of
pair~13, written short: \(\tfrac12 + 4\).}
\pair{06-data-structures/string-unicode/17}{\texttt{\textbackslash\%} completes
\(x_a^b\), which reads as a power of a subscripted symbol;
\texttt{\textbackslash\&} and \texttt{\textbackslash+} give \mcode{Overscript} and
\mcode{Underscript}.}
\end{codepairs}

The pieces combine naturally with the associations of \S\ref{sec:ds-associations}.
First, a small configuration file: drop the comment lines, then let one pattern with
two named pieces turn each \texttt{key = value} line into a rule.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/string-worked/1}{The raw text, with inconsistent spacing

\pair{06-data-structures/string-worked/2}{Split into lines and keep those that do not

\end{codepairs}

Second, word frequencies. Extract the runs of word characters, which discards

\begin{codepairs}

\section{NDArrays and packed arrays}
\label{sec:ds-packed}

Everything so far treated a list as a tree of independent expression nodes. For
symbolic work that is exactly right. For numeric work it is ruinous: a list of a
million reals is a million separate nodes, and the evaluator sweeps every argument of
every call on every pass, so merely \emph{holding} a large list costs time on each
operation, whether or not anything is computed. Mathilda's answer is the
\emph{packed array}\index{packed array}---the same list, stored as one dense
machine-precision buffer instead of a million nodes---and the discipline that keeps
that storage change completely invisible.

\subsection{The invisible buffer}
\label{ssec:ds-invisible}

\B{ToNDArray} packs a list into a buffer and hands it back looking exactly like the
list it was. The only tool that can tell the difference is \B{NDArrayQ}, which asks
about storage rather than about value.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/packing/1}{\B{ToNDArray} returns what looks like an ordinary
list of reals---because that is what it still is.}
\pair{06-data-structures/packing/2}{Its head is \texttt{List}, it is a list, and it
is not an atom: every structural predicate answers as it would for the unpacked
list.}
\pair{06-data-structures/packing/3}{Only \B{NDArrayQ} reveals that it is packed.}
\pair{06-data-structures/packing/4}{And it is \B{SameQ} to the plain list---same
value, same identity.}
\pair{06-data-structures/packing/5}{\B{DataType} reports the element type\index{data
type!machine (dtype)} of the buffer: a \texttt{"float64"} of doubles.}
\end{codepairs}

The contract is exact: packing changes representation and \emph{nothing else}---not
the value, not an element's head, not the printed form, not the ordering. So the
representation Mathilda picks on its own can never be observed. That single rule is
what the rest of this section is really about.

\subsection{The exactness contract}
\label{ssec:ds-exact}

The sharpest test of the contract\index{packed array!exactness contract} is exact
integers. A list of integers packs to an \texttt{int64} buffer---and every element
must still come back an \emph{Integer}, not a real, because \(1 \ne 1.0\) is a
difference the buffer is forbidden to introduce.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/packing/7}{An all-integer list packs to \texttt{"int64"},
not to floats.}
\pair{06-data-structures/packing/8}{And an element read back out has head
\texttt{Integer}, exactly as from the unpacked list.}
\pair{06-data-structures/packing/9}{A reduction stays exact: the sum of \(1\ldots
1000\) is the integer \(500500\), computed on the buffer but with no loss of
exactness.}
\pair{06-data-structures/packing/10}{Where a buffer cannot hold the exact answer, it
abandons: \(\mathit{Range}[10]/2\) is a list of exact \B{Rational}s, which no
machine buffer represents, so the ordinary list gives them.}
\pair{06-data-structures/packing/11}{And a list holding a \B{Rational} refuses to
pack at all---\B{ToNDArray} returns it unchanged rather than round it.}
\end{codepairs}

The rule is the same one that governs the numeric tower everywhere in Mathilda:
compute the buffer answer only when it is \emph{the} answer the interpreter would
give, and otherwise fall back to the slower, exact path. An overflow past
\texttt{int64}, a rational, a radical, a symbol---each makes the buffer decline and
the ordinary list answer.

\calloutnote{Pitfall}{Only \B{ToNDArray} widens a mixed integer/real list to a float
buffer, because you explicitly asked for a single machine type. \emph{Automatic}
packing never does: it leaves \(\{1, 2, 3.\}\) an ordinary list, since silently
turning the exact \(1\) into \(1.0\) would be an observable change and the system is
forbidden to make one on its own. The asymmetry is the contract in action---a
representation you request may cost exactness; one the system chooses for you never
may.}

\subsection{When packing happens}
\label{ssec:ds-auto}

You rarely call \B{ToNDArray} yourself. The common case is that a \emph{producer}
builds a packed array directly: \B{Range}, \B{ConstantArray}, \B{Table} with a
machine-real body, and a handful of others write the buffer without ever
materializing the nodes---which is where most of the win is, since building a million
nodes and then packing them costs far more than writing a million doubles.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/packing/12}{\B{Range} of reals packs automatically once the
result is big enough.}
\pair{06-data-structures/packing/13}{A list written out literally in the source never
packs, at any size---there is no producer to opt in, and a source literal is left
exactly as typed.}
\pair{06-data-structures/packing/14}{The master switch \B{\$AutoArrayPacking} turns
automatic packing off.}
\pair{06-data-structures/packing/15}{The same switch turns it back on. It never
touches an explicit \B{ToNDArray}---that is the user asking, not the system
guessing.}
\end{codepairs}

The distinction in the middle pairs surprises people: \mcode{Range[1., 300.]} packs
but the identical-looking literal \(\{1., 2., 3., 4.\}\) does not. Packing is opt-in
per producer, and a hand-written literal has no producer to opt in. A threshold of
four elements (the size of a \(2\times2\) matrix, so that any matrix packs) keeps the
mechanism from paying its own overhead on tiny lists.

\subsection{Complex buffers}
\label{ssec:ds-complex}

Reals and integers are not the only machine numbers. A list of machine complex
numbers packs to a \texttt{"complex64"}\index{data type!complex64} buffer---two
doubles per element, real and imaginary parts interleaved---and \B{RandomComplex}
writes one directly, just as \B{RandomReal} writes a \texttt{"float64"} buffer. The
exactness contract decides the borderline cases exactly as it did for integers and
reals.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/packing-complex/1}{A thousand random points in the square
\([-1,1]^2\) of the complex plane.}
\pair{06-data-structures/packing-complex/2}{\B{RandomComplex} filled a packed
\texttt{"complex64"} buffer.}
\pair{06-data-structures/packing-complex/3}{A \mcode{Table} whose body is a machine
complex number builds \texttt{c}.}
\pair{06-data-structures/packing-complex/4}{It packs the same way, into a
\texttt{"complex64"} buffer.}
\pair{06-data-structures/packing-complex/5}{Element-wise functions keep it packed:
\(e^{ic}\) is computed on the buffer.}
\pair{06-data-structures/packing-complex/6}{One \emph{real} element among the complex

\end{codepairs}

\calloutnote{Performance}{Before complex lists packed, every complex array fell to
the element-by-element interpreter path even where a machine kernel existed. On this
build, \mcode{Sin} of a million random complex numbers takes about \(7\) milliseconds on
the \texttt{"complex64"} buffer and about \(1.8\) seconds on the same values unpacked
with \mcode{FromPackedArray}---two orders of magnitude (the ratio varies between runs, from about \(170\) to \(270\) here), with bit-identical results.}

The same buffers feed the special functions of several arguments. A listable
function such as \B{Hypergeometric2F1} or \B{LerchPhi} maps a packed array in a single
pass \emph{whichever} argument the array occupies, and returns a packed result whose
values equal those of the boxed list.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/packing-complex/8}{Ten packed sample points.}

\({}_2F_1(1,2;3;x)\): the result stays packed.}
\pair{06-data-structures/packing-complex/10}{As the first argument of
\(\Phi(x,2,1)\): packed again.}
\pair{06-data-structures/packing-complex/11}{Even as a \emph{parameter}, \({}_2F_1(x,2;3;\tfrac12)\)
maps over the buffer.}
\pair{06-data-structures/packing-complex/12}{And the buffer answers agree with calling
the function on each element in turn.}
\end{codepairs}

\subsection{The visible NDArray}
\label{ssec:ds-visible}

The packed list is one of two faces of the same storage. The other is the
\emph{visible} \B{NDArray}---the identical dense buffer, but wearing its own head, so
it announces itself as a distinct, purely numeric object modelled on numpy's
\texttt{ndarray}. The two share every byte of representation and differ only in what
they claim to be.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/ndarray/2}{\B{NDArray} prints wrapped in its own head.}
\pair{06-data-structures/ndarray/3}{That head is \texttt{NDArray}, not
\texttt{List}.}
\pair{06-data-structures/ndarray/4}{So it is \emph{not} \B{SameQ} to the plain
list---the head is part of a value's identity.}
\pair{06-data-structures/ndarray/5}{\B{NDArrayQ} is \texttt{True} for both faces: it
asks about storage.}
\pair{06-data-structures/ndarray/6}{\B{PackedArrayQ} is the narrower test---\texttt{True}
only for the packed \emph{list}, \texttt{False} for the visible \texttt{NDArray},
which is an atom rather than a list that happens to be packed.}
\pair{06-data-structures/ndarray/7}{\B{Normal} converts a visible array back to an
ordinary nested list.}
\end{codepairs}

The difference is one of intent. A packed list is Mathilda saying ``this is an
ordinary list; I have merely chosen to store it efficiently.'' A visible
\B{NDArray} is you saying ``this is a numeric array; treat it as one''---so it
combines with scalars by numpy-style broadcasting, refuses symbolic operands, and
stays a closed system under the linear-algebra routines. \B{ToPackedArray} and
\B{FromPackedArray} are Mathematica's names for \B{ToNDArray} and \B{FromNDArray},
the same builtins under a second registration.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/ndarray/8}{\B{ToPackedArray} widens a mixed integer/real
list to a single \texttt{"float64"} buffer.}
\pair{06-data-structures/ndarray/9}{The integers become doubles, since one
buffer holds one machine type.}
\pair{06-data-structures/ndarray/11}{A derived array stays packed: adding a scalar to
a packed list gives a packed list.}
\pair{06-data-structures/ndarray/12}{So does mapping an elementary function over
it---\B{Sin} of a packed buffer is a packed buffer.}
\pair{06-data-structures/ndarray/13}{And \B{N} widens a packed \texttt{int64} buffer
to a packed \texttt{float64} one in a single pass, rather than boxing a million
reals.}
\pair{06-data-structures/ndarray/15}{The visible \B{NDArray} carries its rank and

\end{codepairs}

\subsection{The transparency gate}
\label{ssec:ds-gate}

If a packed list can flow into any operation, what stops an operation that knows
nothing about buffers---a pattern match, a \B{Cases} scan, a user-defined rule---from
reading garbage out of one? The answer is the \emph{transparency
gate}\index{packed array!transparency gate}, and it is the mechanism that makes the
whole abstraction safe.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/gate/2}{Start with a packed range.}
\pair{06-data-structures/gate/3}{\B{Cases}---which has no idea packing exists---filters
it with a pattern and gets exactly the right answer.}
\pair{06-data-structures/gate/4}{\B{Reverse} understands buffers, so it reverses in
place.}
\pair{06-data-structures/gate/5}{It keeps the result packed.}
\pair{06-data-structures/gate/6}{And the buffer path and the ordinary path agree on
the answer, by construction.}
\end{codepairs}

\calloutnote{Under the hood}{The gate lives in the evaluator (\texttt{src/eval.c}),
just before a call reaches its builtin. Its rule inverts the usual default: a packed
buffer is \emph{materialized} back into ordinary nodes for any head that has not
explicitly declared itself packed-aware on the \texttt{AWARE} list in
\texttt{src/pack.c}. So \B{Cases}, \B{Position}, \B{ReplaceAll}, pattern matching,
and every user rule are correct with no code of their own---they simply never see a
buffer. A head like \B{Reverse}, \B{Total}, \B{Sin}, or \B{Dot} that \emph{has} opted
in and been checked receives the buffer intact and runs its C kernel. Forgetting to
opt a head in costs a materialization---a slowdown---never a wrong answer. The
storage overlay is arranged so that a walker which slipped past the gate and iterated
a buffer as if it were a node array would segfault immediately rather than return
something plausible, turning the dangerous silent-wrong-answer class into a loud
crash.}

\calloutnote{Performance}{The payoff is large and the design record measures it on a
\(10^6\)-element list of reals: \B{Length} falls from about \(30.6\)
milliseconds on an ordinary list to \(2\) microseconds packed, \B{Total} from
\(95.8\) milliseconds to \(4.0\), and \B{Sin} from \(320\) milliseconds to
\(16.8\)---the \(30\)-nanosecond-per-element-per-pass evaluator sweep replaced by a
tight loop over contiguous memory (\texttt{docs/design/packed\_arrays.md}). Because a
packed buffer is exactly the representation \B{Compile} wants, these same arrays cross
the compiled-code boundary without being unpacked and repacked at each end---the
subject of Chapter~\ref{ch:compilation}.}

\section{SparseArray}
\label{sec:ds-sparse}

A packed array saves the cost of one node per element. It still stores every element,
and many of the arrays that matter are almost all zeros: the matrix of a finite-difference
stencil, the adjacency matrix of a graph, a term--document table. A
\(10^6\times10^6\) tridiagonal matrix has three million nonzero entries and
\(10^{12}\) zeros, and a dense buffer for it would need eight terabytes. The
\B{SparseArray}\index{sparse array} stores only the entries that differ from a
\emph{background} value\index{sparse array!background}, which is usually \(0\), and
records where they are.

\subsection{Building one}
\label{ssec:ds-sparse-build}

The basic constructor takes a list of rules from positions to values, and a list of
dimensions. Like an association, the result is an atom: \B{AtomQ} is
\texttt{True} and it prints as a summary, the number of stored entries and the shape.
Yet \B{Length} and \B{Dimensions} answer exactly as they would for the dense
array it stands for. \B{Normal} gives that dense array, and \texttt{ArrayRules} gives the
rules back.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/sparse/1}{Three explicit entries in a \(3\times3\) array,
one of them symbolic. The output is a summary: three stored entries, shape
\(\{3,3\}\).}
\pair{06-data-structures/sparse/2}{It is an atom with head \texttt{SparseArray},
but its length and dimensions are those of the matrix.}
\pair{06-data-structures/sparse/3}{\B{Normal} fills in the background.}
\pair{06-data-structures/sparse/4}{\texttt{ArrayRules} lists the explicit entries and
ends with the background rule \texttt{\{\_, \_\} -> 0}, so that
\texttt{SparseArray[ArrayRules[s]]} rebuilds \(s\).}
\pair{06-data-structures/sparse/5}{\B{InputForm} shows the stored form: the shape,
the background, the row pointers \(\{0,1,2,3\}\), the column of each entry, and the
values.}
\pair{06-data-structures/sparse/6}{\B{Part} reads an element directly.}
\pair{06-data-structures/sparse/7}{A property call reads the structure itself: here
the positions of the stored entries.}
\end{codepairs}

A rule's left-hand side need not be a single position. A third argument changes the
background. A pattern fills every position it matches, and the pattern
\texttt{\{i\_, i\_\}} walks only the diagonal. \texttt{Band} fills a diagonal starting at a
given position. And \B{SparseArray} applied to an ordinary array keeps its nonzero
entries.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/sparse/8}{A vector of length five with background \(1\) and
a single \(7\).}
\pair{06-data-structures/sparse/9}{A pattern rule: \(i\) on the diagonal.}
\pair{06-data-structures/sparse/10}{Three \texttt{Band}s give the second-difference
matrix, the discrete \(-d^2/dx^2\).}
\pair{06-data-structures/sparse/11}{A dense matrix with two nonzero entries becomes a
sparse array with two stored entries.}
\end{codepairs}

When several rules name the same position, the first one wins. An entry equal to the
background is not stored. A sparse array's elements cannot themselves be lists. The
full list of constructor forms, and the messages for malformed input, are on the
\B{SparseArray} reference page.

\calloutnote{Under the hood}{A SparseArray is a native atom, \texttt{EXPR\_SPARSE}
(\texttt{src/sparse/}), and it is always held in one standard form: the one that
\B{InputForm} printed above. The storage is compressed sparse row
(CSR)\index{sparse array!compressed sparse row}, generalized to any rank. A row-pointer
vector \texttt{rowptr} (0-based) marks where each index of the \emph{first} dimension
begins in the entry list. For each entry, \texttt{colidx} holds its remaining
coordinates as a 1-based tuple, sorted within each slice. The values sit in one typed
machine buffer when they are all machine integers, all reals or all complex numbers,
and are boxed only otherwise. Column indices are 32-bit when the dimensions allow it.
Because the standard form is unique, \B{SameQ} is equality of arrays, and a sparse
array can be a key in an association or an element of a set. An array derived
element-wise from another (\(2s\), \(s+1\), \(\sin s\), \texttt{N[s]}) shares its
parent's index structure by reference count and allocates only a new value vector.}

\subsection{Working with a sparse array}
\label{ssec:ds-sparse-ops}

A sparse array is an atom, so it meets the same problem a packed buffer does: most
builtins know nothing about its storage. The answer is the same as well, a
\emph{transparency gate}\index{sparse array!transparency gate}
(\texttt{src/sparse/sparse\_gate.c}). Every builtin accepts a SparseArray, and for
each head the gate settles, call shape by call shape, which of four ways the head sees
it:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{as its dense form}: the head answers what it would answer on
\texttt{Normal[s]} (\B{Accumulate}, \B{Det}, \B{Tally}, \B{Union});
\item \emph{as an array that stays sparse}: the answer is a SparseArray again
(\B{Sort}, \B{Chop}, \B{PadRight});
\item \emph{at level 1}: \B{Map}, \B{Apply}, \B{Scan}, and \B{Select} see the rows,
which are themselves sparse arrays;
\item \emph{as an atom}: \B{Count}, \B{Cases}, \B{MemberQ}, \B{ReplaceAll}, and user
functions see the SparseArray itself, not its entries.
\end{itemize}
The rule \(\mathtt{Normal}[f[s]] = f[\mathtt{Normal}[s]]\) holds for every head in the
first three groups, and a stress tool (\texttt{tools/sparse\_stress.py}) re-checks it
on random arrays.

The heads that matter for numerical work do not go through the dense form at all. They
have native kernels that read the stored entries:
\begin{itemize}
\item every \texttt{Listable} head (\B{Plus}, \B{Times}, \B{Power}, \B{Sin},
\B{Exp}, \ldots), applied to the explicit values and once to the background;
\item the reductions \B{Total}, \B{Mean}, \B{Max}, \B{Min}, \B{Norm}, \B{Tr}, and
\B{Equal}, which add in the background arithmetically, once for each implicit
position;
\item the structural heads \B{Transpose}, \B{Flatten}, \B{Take}, \B{Join},
\B{Diagonal}, \texttt{KroneckerProduct}, \B{ArrayFlatten}, and the padding and
triangularizing heads, each at a cost of \(O(\text{rows}+\text{nnz})\);
\item \B{Map} at the leaf level, which visits the stored values and the
background in \(O(\text{nnz})\);
\item \B{Dot} in every combination: sparse matrix times vector (SpMV), sparse times
dense matrix (SpMM), and sparse times sparse (SpGEMM), together with \B{MatrixPower}.
\end{itemize}
\B{IdentityMatrix} builds a sparse identity directly when its second argument is
\texttt{SparseArray}.

The session below builds the \(10^6\times10^6\) second-difference matrix and works on
it. None of these calls ever forms the \(10^{12}\)-element dense array.

\B{Sort}, \B{Union}, \B{Split}, \B{Gather}, \B{Ordering}, and \B{Fold} compute an

\B{Do}, and \B{Table} visit the elements in order, background included, where the
reference visits the stored values first.}

arrays, where scipy copies them. A transcendental function runs the same ND kernel
the packed surface uses, over the value vector. Construction is a single stable radix
sort on a composite row--column key. And SpGEMM is a two-pass Gustavson product,
threaded, with a typed kernel for integers, reals, and complex numbers and an exact
fallback for overflow and symbolic entries.}

\section{LinkedList: the mutable list}
\label{sec:ds-linkedlist}

Every value in this chapter so far has been immutable. A transformation returns a new
value and leaves the old one alone, and that is what lets the evaluator share
subexpressions freely. The price is paid when a program builds a list one element at a
time. \texttt{LinkedList}\index{LinkedList@\mcode{LinkedList}}\index{linked list} is the exception: a list object that is
changed in place.

\subsection{Why it exists}
\label{ssec:ds-ll-why}

The natural way to accumulate results is \texttt{AppendTo}. On a List, every
\texttt{AppendTo} copies the whole list into a new one with one more element, so
\(n\) appends copy \(1+2+\cdots+n\approx n^2/2\) elements in all. The cost of the loop
is \(O(n^2)\)\index{AppendTo@\mcode{AppendTo}!quadratic cost}. The usual way out is
\B{Sow} and \B{Reap}, which is \(O(n)\). But the accumulator then exists only inside
the \texttt{Reap}. You cannot keep it in a variable, look at it halfway through, or
pass it to a helper that adds to it. A LinkedList gives \(O(1)\) appends and removals
at both ends and \(O(1)\) indexing, and it is an ordinary value that a variable holds.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist-perf/3}{Fifty thousand appends to a List, with
auto-compilation switched off so that only the data structure differs. The
time is in seconds.}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist-perf/4}{The same loop on a LinkedList, more than
ten times faster here, and the ratio grows linearly with \(n\).}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist-perf/5}{Both hold the same elements.}
\end{codepairs}

\subsection{The object}
\label{ssec:ds-ll-object}

\texttt{LinkedList[]} creates an empty list. \texttt{LinkedList[\(\mathit{list}\)]}
creates one holding the elements of a List, a packed array, or an \B{NDArray}; a packed
argument is copied buffer to buffer, never unpacked. Like a SparseArray, the result is
an atom\index{linked list!atom}: \B{ListQ} is \texttt{False}, \texttt{\{\_\_\}} does
not match it, and it prints as \texttt{LinkedList[<\(n\)>]} in every form, because a
mutable object has no literal that could be read back. \B{Normal} returns the
elements as a List, packed when they are all machine numbers of one kind.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist/1}{An empty list.}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist/2}{\texttt{AppendTo} grows it in place. The value of
\texttt{ll} is the same object as before, now holding five squares.}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist/3}{An atom, not a List, with head
\texttt{LinkedList}. \B{Length} is \(O(1)\).}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist/4}{\B{Normal} gives the elements.}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist/5}{\B{Part} reads one element in \(O(1)\). A span
gives a \emph{new} LinkedList of the selected elements.}
\end{codepairs}

What sets a LinkedList apart from every other value is that it has \emph{reference
semantics}\index{linked list!reference semantics}. Assigning it to a second variable,
or passing it to a function, shares the one object: a change made through either name
is seen through both. It is the only shared mutable value in the system. Equality is
therefore identity. \B{SameQ} is \texttt{True} only for the same object, and two
distinct lists with equal elements are not \texttt{SameQ}. Compare contents with
\texttt{Normal}, and copy with \texttt{LinkedList[\(\mathit{ll}\)]}.

\begin{codepairs}

list.}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist/7}{\texttt{PrependTo} through the second name\ldots}

\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist/9}{They are the same object.}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist/10}{\texttt{LinkedList[ll]} makes an independent
shallow copy. \texttt{Push} on the copy leaves the original at six elements.}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist/11}{Two separate lists are never \B{SameQ}, even
with equal elements\ldots}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist/12}{\ldots so compare their \B{Normal} forms.}
\end{codepairs}

\calloutnote{Pitfall}{Aliasing is deliberate. Three models were considered (design
record, \texttt{LINKED\_LIST\_DESIGN.md} \S3.4). Copying on \B{Set} alone was rejected
because values also travel by argument passing, \B{With}, rule replacement, and
return values, and \texttt{f[ll]} would mutate while \texttt{b = ll; Push[b, 1]} did
not. Copy-on-write value semantics was rejected because a helper could then never add
to its caller's list, and because ``nothing else refers to it'' is fragile:
\texttt{Out[n]} and memo tables hold references and would turn an apparent \(O(1)\)
append into a silent \(O(n)\) copy. With pointer semantics every assignment is
\(O(1)\) and the costs are predictable, but you have to copy explicitly when you want
a copy.}

The mutating heads are few, and only they change a list in place: \texttt{AppendTo},
\texttt{PrependTo}, \texttt{Push}, \texttt{Pop}, \texttt{PopFirst}, and a \B{Part} assignment
\texttt{ll[[\(i\)]] = \(v\)}. All six are \(O(1)\). \texttt{Push}\index{Push@\mcode{Push}} appends; \texttt{Pop}\index{Pop@\mcode{Pop}} removes
and returns the last element, and \texttt{PopFirst}\index{PopFirst@\mcode{PopFirst}} the first. Push and Pop together make a
last-in first-out \emph{stack}\index{stack}; Push and PopFirst make a first-in
first-out \emph{queue}\index{queue}.

\texttt{Push}, \texttt{Pop}, and \texttt{PopFirst} hold their first argument, and they also accept a
variable holding a plain List, which they rebind as \texttt{AppendTo} does. That costs
\(O(n)\), but it means code written for a stack runs on either representation.

\subsection{Representation and complexity}
\label{ssec:ds-ll-repr}

Despite its name, a LinkedList is not a chain of one cell per element. A chain of
cells would give \(O(1)\) appends but \(O(n)\) indexing, one allocation per element,
and poor locality. A full \B{Part} specification needs indexing to be fast.

\calloutnote{Under the hood}{The storage (\texttt{src/linkedlist.\{c,h\}}) is a deque
of two \emph{runs} of chunks\index{linked list!chunked storage}: a front run that grows
leftward under \texttt{PrependTo}, and a back run that grows rightward under \texttt{AppendTo}.
In each run, chunk \(k\) holds \(8\cdot2^k\) elements, so the capacities double, and a
directory of chunk pointers (64 entries, enough for any addressable length, allocated
on demand) reaches each chunk without following links. Element \(j\) of a run lies in
chunk \(k=\lfloor\log_2(j/8+1)\rfloor\), which is one count-leading-zeros instruction,
at offset \(j-8(2^k-1)\). A logical index \(i\) is in the front run if \(i\) is less
than the front run's length, and in the back run otherwise. Growth writes into the
last chunk of a run and links a new chunk when that one is full, so \emph{elements
never move}: there is no reallocate-and-copy step as in a doubling vector, and \(n\)
appends make only \(O(\log n)\) allocations. Removal at an end advances a start
offset and frees a chunk that empties, and a queue's consumed prefix is reclaimed, so
memory stays proportional to the live elements: \(10^6\) push--pop pairs on a
ten-element queue hold under 4 KB. The storage is \emph{typed}\index{linked
list!typed storage}. While every element is a machine integer, real, or complex number
of one kind (the same rule that decides whether a List packs), the chunks hold raw
\texttt{int64}, \texttt{double}, or \((\mathrm{re},\mathrm{im})\) pairs. The first
element that does not conform converts the list to boxed \texttt{Expr} pointers, once,
in \(O(n)\), and a list never converts back. Typed storage is what lets \B{Normal}
return a packed array by \texttt{memcpy}, and what lets machine kernels and compiled
code read the slots directly. Equality and hashing use a stable per-list id rather
than the contents. A hash cached in a parent List or Association therefore never goes
stale when the list is mutated, and the evaluator never caches a result computed from
a LinkedList.}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
operation & LinkedList & List \\
\midrule
\texttt{AppendTo}, \texttt{PrependTo}, \texttt{Push} & \(O(1)\) amortized & \(O(n)\) \\
\texttt{Pop}, \texttt{PopFirst} & \(O(1)\) & \(O(n)\) (rebinds \texttt{Most}/\texttt{Rest}) \\
\texttt{ll[[i]]}, \texttt{ll[[i]] = v}, \B{First}, \B{Last}, \B{Length} & \(O(1)\) & \(O(1)\) read, \(O(n)\) write \\
span, list, or \texttt{All} selection & \(O(k)\) for \(k\) selected & \(O(k)\) \\
\B{Insert}, \B{Delete} in the middle & \(O(n)\) & \(O(n)\) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The last row is what a cons list would do better: splicing in the middle copies, as
it does for a List.

\calloutnote{Performance}{On \(10^6\) machine reals the design record measures a
LinkedList against a packed List. \B{Insert}, \B{Delete}, and \texttt{ReplacePart} take
\(0.8\) ms against \(86\), \(85\), and \(214\) ms, because they copy whole chunk spans
with \texttt{memcpy}. \B{Reverse} takes \(1.6\) ms against \(3.4\), and \B{Join}
\(4.5\) against \(7.6\). \B{Sin} and \texttt{ll + 1.} run the packed List's own
kernels on the slots, so they cost the same, \(1.39\) against \(1.35\) ms and \(1.21\)
against \(1.17\) ms. \B{Take}, \B{Drop}, \B{Map}, and \B{Select} are within a few
percent. The weak spot is \B{Partition}, whose pieces are themselves LinkedLists:
\texttt{Partition[ll, 2]} over \(10^6\) elements builds half a million small objects,
and takes \(0.24\) s against \(0.004\) s for the packed List.}

\subsection{It works everywhere a List does}
\label{ssec:ds-ll-everywhere}

A LinkedList is an atom, but no head treats it as an opaque one. Every builtin that
works on a List works on a LinkedList and gives the same answer it would give for
\texttt{Normal[ll]}. Machine numbers agree to the bit, because a typed list runs the
very kernels a packed List reaches. The rules:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Closure}\index{linked list!closure}. A head that returns a List for a List
returns a new LinkedList for a LinkedList: \B{Sort}, \B{Select}, \B{Cases},
\B{Accumulate}, the Listable arithmetic, and \B{Partition}, whose pieces are
LinkedLists too. Fixed-shape records stay Lists: \B{Position} index lists, the pairs
of \B{Tally}, \B{MinMax}, \B{Dimensions}. Scalars stay scalars.
\item \emph{A rank-1 container}. \B{Level}, \B{Position}, \B{Apply}, \B{Dimensions},
and the iterators see the elements one level down, and a LinkedList of equal-length
lists is a matrix.
\item \emph{Snapshots}. A head that runs your code per element (\B{Map}, \B{Select},
\B{Scan}, \B{Fold}) or an iterator \texttt{\{i, ll\}} reads the elements the list had
when it began, so a callback that changes the list changes neither the walk nor its
length.
\end{itemize}

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist-everywhere/2}{\B{Sort} of a LinkedList is a new
LinkedList\ldots}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist-everywhere/3}{\ldots holding the sorted
elements.}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist-everywhere/4}{Reductions give scalars, exact where
the elements are.}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist-everywhere/5}{\B{Position} returns an index list,
a record, so it stays a List.}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist-everywhere/6}{\B{Select} gives a LinkedList of
the even elements.}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist-everywhere/7}{\B{Partition} gives a LinkedList of
LinkedLists, here made Lists at both levels to print.}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist-everywhere/8}{Listable arithmetic threads over the
elements, as over a List.}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist-everywhere/12}{The iterator takes its snapshot
when the loop begins, so the loop runs six times and appends six elements, even
though the list grows as it runs.}
\end{codepairs}

None of this converts the list to a List. A head reads the chunks one slot at a time,
or gathers a typed list's slots into a raw scratch buffer when an algorithm needs
random access (a sort, an FFT). Only \B{Normal} builds a List of the elements. This
\emph{no-element-arrays invariant}\index{linked list!no-element-arrays invariant} is
checkable: \texttt{Internal`LinkedListConversionCount[]} counts every conversion.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist-everywhere/10}{A sort, a sum, a vectorized
\B{Sin}, and a partition: the counter does not move.}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist-everywhere/11}{\B{Normal} is the one head that
converts, and it counts.}
\end{codepairs}

\calloutnote{Under the hood}{The invariant is enforced by \texttt{make
check-linkedlist} (\texttt{tools/check\_linkedlist\_surface.py}). It runs every
\texttt{System`} head over List and LinkedList fixtures in seven call shapes, and checks
each LinkedList call four ways: the answer against \texttt{H[Normal[ll]]}, the
conversion counter, that the argument is unchanged, and that the messages match the
List call's. When the gate was introduced it reported 552 findings. When the work
finished (v0.6664) it reported 12, and those 12 are deliberate: a LinkedList used as
a level, position, or dimension \emph{specification} (\B{Delete}, \B{Reverse},
\B{Array}, \B{ConstantArray}, \B{RotateLeft}, the range of \B{Integrate}), and as the
variable list of \B{CoefficientRules}, \B{CoefficientArrays}, and the \B{Minimize}
family. The machine engines reach the slots through container-free \texttt{NDOperand}
cores (\texttt{src/ndarray.h}). Those are the cores the packed-array entry points now
wrap, which is why a LinkedList and a packed List give bit-identical answers. Matrix
linear algebra (\B{Det}, \B{Inverse}, the decompositions), graphs, machine learning,
plotting, and images still take Lists.}

\subsection{Compiled loops}
\label{ssec:ds-ll-compile}

A LinkedList of machine integers, reals, or complex numbers (one kind per list)
compiles\index{linked list!in compiled code}. Inside \B{Compile} it can be a
\B{Module} local or an argument declared \texttt{\{b, \_LinkedList, \_Real\}}. The
mutators, the reads (\B{Length}, \B{Part}, \B{First}, \B{Last}, \B{Total},
\B{Normal}), and the copying heads (\B{Most}, \B{Rest}, \B{Reverse}, \B{Take},
\B{Drop}, \B{Append}, \B{Prepend}, \B{Join}) all lower to bytecode. Reference
semantics carry over: an argument is the caller's own list, mutated in place.
Auto-compilation goes further. An interpreted \B{Do}, \B{For}, or \B{While} whose body
works on a global LinkedList compiles as one program, with no change to the code.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist-perf/6}{A million appends, interpreted (still
with \B{\$AutoCompilation} off). The time is in seconds.}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist-perf/8}{The same loop with auto-compilation on:
the whole \B{Do} runs as bytecode.}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist-perf/9}{About an order of magnitude faster. The
design record measured \(37\) ms against \(0.5\) s.}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist-perf/10}{A compiled function that takes a
LinkedList of reals and pushes \(k\) squares onto it.}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist-perf/11}{Calling it on an empty list\ldots}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist-perf/12}{\ldots fills the caller's list in place.}
\end{codepairs}

\calloutnote{Under the hood}{Two rules keep compiled code exactly as correct as the
interpreter. First, a list has one element type and it is never widened: widening
integers and reals to reals would turn the interpreter's mixed \(\{1, 2.5\}\) into
\(\{1., 2.5\}\), so a body that pushes two types runs interpreted. Second, a compiled
call can be abandoned partway (an empty \texttt{Pop}, an index out of range, an integer
\B{Total} past \texttt{int64}), and the interpreter then re-runs it from the start.
That is sound only if the abandoned run left no trace, so every push, pop, and write
to a list created \emph{before} the call is recorded in an undo log and rolled back
first. The caller's list ends exactly as one interpreted run leaves it, with the
interpreter's message. For the same reason a per-element auto-compiled body (\B{Table},
\B{Map}, \B{Sum}) may read a global list but not change it, since it can be re-run
from its first element. Compilation in general is the subject of
Chapter~\ref{ch:compilation}.}

\subsection{Worked examples: a stack and a queue}
\label{ssec:ds-ll-worked}

Two classic algorithms show the shared, mutable list at work. Each passes the list to
a helper that changes it. That would be impossible with a List, and awkward with
\B{Sow} and \B{Reap}.

The first checks whether the brackets in a string are balanced. An opening bracket is
pushed on a stack, and a closing bracket must match the opener on top, which it pops.
The string is balanced if no closer fails to match and the stack is empty at the end.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist-worked/6}{Each closer, keyed to the opener it
must match.}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist-worked/7}{One character. A closer pops the stack
and compares; an opener is pushed. \texttt{st} is the caller's list, so the push and
pop happen there.}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist-worked/8}{\B{AllTrue} stops at the first
mismatch; then the stack must be empty.}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist-worked/9}{Properly nested; crossed; and two
openers never closed.}
\end{codepairs}

The second is breadth-first search\index{breadth-first search} on a small directed
graph, given as an association from each vertex to its successors. A queue holds the
frontier. Each vertex taken from the front pushes its unvisited successors onto the
back, one step further from the start, so vertices leave the queue in order of
distance.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist-worked/10}{Six vertices; \(4\) is reached from
both \(2\) and \(3\).}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist-worked/11}{The queue starts with the source. A
vertex seen for the first time gets a distance one more than its parent's, and is
pushed.}
\pair{06-data-structures/linkedlist-worked/12}{The distance of every vertex from
vertex \(1\).}
\end{codepairs}

Every \texttt{Push} and \texttt{PopFirst} here is \(O(1)\), so the search is \(O(V+E)\), as it
should be. Written with a List and \B{Rest}, each dequeue would copy the frontier.


\section*{Where this connects}

Data structures are the substrate the rest of Mathilda stands on. The uniform
expression tree of \S\ref{sec:ds-expressions} is what makes the pattern matcher and
rule engine of Chapter~\ref{ch:programming} possible: a pattern is an expression, a
rule is an expression, and matching one tree against another is the single mechanism
behind \B{Map}, \B{Cases}, \B{ReplaceAll}, and every definition you write. The
levelspecs and structural operators introduced here reappear there as the working
vocabulary of functional programming, and the string patterns of
\S\ref{sec:ds-strings} are that same pattern language turned on text. Associations are the natural output of the
grouping and counting that descriptive statistics (\S\ref{sec:statistics}) runs on,
and their \(O(1)\) keyed store underlies any code that indexes data by name. The
packed-array substrate of \S\ref{sec:ds-packed} is where numeric Mathilda gets its
speed: it feeds the machine-precision linear algebra of \S\ref{sec:linalg}, and it is
half of the story of \B{Compile} (Chapter~\ref{ch:compilation}), the other half being
the bytecode VM that consumes those buffers. The sparse arrays of
\S\ref{sec:ds-sparse} carry the large sparse systems of the linear-algebra chapter
(Section~\ref{sec:linalg-sparse}), and the \texttt{LinkedList} of
\S\ref{sec:ds-linkedlist} is the accumulator to reach for in the procedural loops of
Chapter~\ref{ch:programming}. For the exhaustive options of any
function named here, its reference page is one \texttt{?Name} away at the prompt---and
the full machinery of the evaluator, the matcher, and the transparency gate is laid
out in Chapter~\ref{ch:internals}.
