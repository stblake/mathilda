\section{Linear Algebra}
\label{sec:linalg}

Linear algebra is where symbolic computation and fast numerics meet most
directly, and Mathilda meets them with a single idea: a matrix is not a special
kind of value. It is an ordinary expression---a \B{List} of \B{List}s---so the
generic tools you already know (\B{Part}, \B{Map}, \B{Transpose}) apply to it
without ceremony, and a routine like \B{Det} or \B{Inverse} simply reads the
structure off the tree.\index{matrix!as an expression} That uniformity is one of
the two themes of this section. The other is the split that runs underneath every
function here: the \emph{same} call is answered exactly when its entries are exact
and at the speed of C when they are machine numbers. Hand \B{Inverse} an integer
matrix and you get an exact rational inverse; hand it a matrix of measured reals
and the work goes to LAPACK over a packed buffer. The mathematics you type is the
mathematics that runs, whichever world the data lives in.

We climb from structure to spectrum. First the matrix as an expression---its
shape, its constructors, and the packed-array substrate that makes it
fast---then the products and norms that turn vectors into geometry; the
determinant, inverse, and matrix powers; functions of a matrix such as
\(e^{A}\) and \(\sqrt{A}\); solving linear systems exactly and in
the least-squares sense; rank, row reduction, and the null space, with the
user-facing \texttt{ZeroTest} option that makes exact reduction possible where a
structural pivot would fail; the matrix decompositions (LU, QR, SVD,
Schur, Cholesky, Hessenberg); and the eigenproblem---eigenvalues, eigenvectors,
and the Jordan canonical form. Then come the machine--exact divide that governs
all of it, with arbitrary precision as its third world, and finally sparse
matrices, where a matrix with \(1.6\) billion entries is stored as its two hundred
thousand nonzeros.

\subsection{Vectors and matrices as expressions}
\label{ssec:la-matrices}

A vector is a flat list; a matrix is a list of equal-length rows. Because that is
all a matrix is, the everyday expression tools work on it unchanged, and a
handful of predicates and constructors round out the vocabulary.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{linear-algebra/matrices/1}{A \(3\times3\) matrix is just a list of three
rows.}
\pair{linear-algebra/matrices/2}{\B{Dimensions} reads the shape---here \(3\times3\).}
\pair{linear-algebra/matrices/3}{\B{MatrixQ} confirms it is a rectangular rank-2
array (its siblings \B{VectorQ} and \B{SquareMatrixQ} test the other shapes).}
\pair{linear-algebra/matrices/4}{\B{Part} slices a column: \texttt{m[[All, 2]]} is
the second entry of every row.}
\pair{linear-algebra/matrices/5}{And because rows are ordinary lists, \B{Map}
threads \B{Total} over them to sum each row---no matrix-specific machinery
needed.}
\pair{linear-algebra/matrices/6}{\B{Transpose} exchanges rows and columns.}
\pair{linear-algebra/matrices/7}{\B{ConjugateTranspose} transposes \emph{and}
conjugates---the adjoint \(A^{*}\), the right notion for complex matrices.}
\pair{linear-algebra/matrices/8}{\B{IdentityMatrix} builds the \(n\times n\)
identity.}
\pair{linear-algebra/matrices/9}{\B{DiagonalMatrix} places a list on the
diagonal; the off-diagonal zeros are exact when the diagonal is.}
\pair{linear-algebra/matrices/10}{\B{Diagonal} reads the diagonal back out.}
\end{codepairs}

The lesson of pairs~4 and~5 is worth dwelling on: nothing in \texttt{m[[All, 2]]}
or \texttt{Map[Total, m]} knows it is operating on a ``matrix.'' They are the same
generic operators that act on any expression, and they work here for free because
a matrix \emph{is} an expression. The specialised predicates---\B{SymmetricMatrixQ},
\B{HermitianMatrixQ}, \B{PositiveDefiniteMatrixQ}---layer structural tests on top
of that same representation.

\calloutnote{Under the hood}{Behind the plain list is an invisible fast lane. When
every entry is a machine number, Mathilda may hold the matrix as a
\emph{packed array}\index{packed array}: a flat, row-major buffer of raw
\texttt{double}s carrying its shape on the side, exactly like NumPy's
\texttt{ndarray}, rather than a tree of boxed \texttt{Expr} nodes. \B{Head},
\B{ListQ}, printing, and pattern matching cannot tell a packed list from any
other list---it is the \emph{same value}, only stored densely---so the uniformity
above costs nothing. The visible form of that same storage is \B{NDArray}, which
prints as \texttt{NDArray[\ldots]} and announces itself; the two share every
kernel. Packing and the transparency gate that guards it are covered in
\S\ref{ssec:la-precision}.}

\subsection{Products and norms}
\label{ssec:la-products}

The single operator \B{Dot} (written as an infix period, \texttt{.}) carries the
whole algebra of products: vector--vector, matrix--vector, and matrix--matrix are
one function, contracting the last index of its left argument with the first index
of its right.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{linear-algebra/products/1}{A matrix times a vector---each output entry is a
row dotted with \(\{x, y\}\).}
\pair{linear-algebra/products/2}{Two matrices multiply to a matrix, row-by-column.}
\pair{linear-algebra/products/3}{Two vectors contract to a scalar, the inner
product \(1\cdot4 + 2\cdot5 + 3\cdot6 = 32\).}
\pair{linear-algebra/products/4}{\B{Cross} is the \(3\)-dimensional cross product:
\(\hat x \times \hat y = \hat z\).}
\pair{linear-algebra/products/5}{In general \B{Cross} takes \(n-1\) vectors of
length \(n\); with one \(2\)-vector it returns the perpendicular in the plane.}
\pair{linear-algebra/products/6}{\B{Tr} sums the diagonal---the trace, invariant
under similarity.}
\end{codepairs}

A \emph{norm} measures length. \B{Norm} defaults to the Euclidean (\(2\)-)norm but
takes any \(p\), and \B{Normalize} scales a vector to unit length.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{linear-algebra/products/7}{\B{Norm} of a symbolic vector is the Hermitian
\(2\)-norm \(\sqrt{\sum |x_i|^2}\)---note the \B{Abs}, so it is correct for complex
entries.}
\pair{linear-algebra/products/8}{The \(1\)-norm sums absolute values: \(1+2+3=6\).}
\pair{linear-algebra/products/9}{The \(\infty\)-norm takes the largest,
\(\max\{1,2,3\}=3\).}
\pair{linear-algebra/products/10}{\B{Normalize} divides by the norm, sending
\(\{3,4\}\) to the unit vector \(\{3/5, 4/5\}\).}
\end{codepairs}

The \(p\)-norms interpolate a family: \(p=1\) the taxicab length, \(p=2\) the
straight-line length, and \(p=\infty\) the largest coordinate. That the symbolic
answer in pair~7 uses \(\lvert x\rvert^2\) rather than \(x^2\) is not pedantry---for
a complex vector the length-squared is \(\sum x_i\overline{x_i}\), and writing
\(x^2\) would give the wrong answer off the real axis.

\calloutnote{Performance}{On a machine-precision vector or matrix these route
through LAPACK: the \(2\)-norm uses \texttt{dnrm2}'s running-scale recurrence and
the matrix norms use \texttt{dlange}, so a well-scaled vector whose true norm is
representable never overflows to \texttt{inf}. \texttt{Norm[\{1e200, 1e200\}]}
returns \(1.414\times10^{200}\)---the naive \(\sqrt{x_1^2 + x_2^2}\) would square to
infinity first. Both \B{Norm} and \B{Dot} lower inside \B{Compile}, so a norm or
inner product inside a tight numerical loop runs at buffer speed
(\texttt{src/linalg/norm.c}, \texttt{dot.c}).}

\texttt{Dot} contracts indices; three relatives build bigger objects or split vectors
apart. The \emph{Kronecker product}\index{Kronecker product} \(A\otimes B\) replaces
each entry \(a_{ij}\) of \(A\) by the block \(a_{ij}B\); the \emph{tensor
product}\index{tensor product} keeps every index of every factor; and a
\emph{projection} splits a vector into the part along a direction and the part
perpendicular to it. \texttt{Orthogonalize}\index{Orthogonalize@\mcode{Orthogonalize}} turns a list of vectors into an orthonormal
one by the Gram--Schmidt process\index{Gram-Schmidt process}.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{linear-algebra/products2/1}{\texttt{KroneckerProduct}\index{KroneckerProduct@\mcode{KroneckerProduct}} of two \(2\times2\) matrices
is \(4\times4\): each entry of the first scales a copy of the second (here a swap
matrix, so each block is a reflected copy).}
\pair{linear-algebra/products2/2}{Of two vectors it is the column-times-row outer
product, a matrix.}
\pair{linear-algebra/products2/3}{\texttt{TensorProduct}\index{TensorProduct@\mcode{TensorProduct}} is the full outer product,
symbolic entries and all.}
\pair{linear-algebra/products2/4}{The rank of a tensor product is the sum of the
ranks, so three vectors give a \(2\times3\times2\) array.}
\pair{linear-algebra/products2/5}{\texttt{Projection}\index{Projection@\mcode{Projection}} of \(\{1,1\}\) onto \(\{3,4\}\) is
\(\frac{u\cdot v}{v\cdot v}\,v = \frac{7}{25}\{3,4\}\), exactly.}
\pair{linear-algebra/products2/6}{What is left over is perpendicular to \(\{3,4\}\):
\(3\cdot\frac{4}{25} - 4\cdot\frac{3}{25} = 0\).}
\pair{linear-algebra/products2/7}{\texttt{Orthogonalize} returns an orthonormal basis,
with exact radicals for exact input.}
\pair{linear-algebra/products2/8}{A dependent vector has nothing left once its
projections are removed, and becomes a zero vector, so the output still lines up
with the input.}
\end{codepairs}

Gram--Schmidt is one projection after another: each new vector loses its
projections onto the vectors already accepted, and what is left is normalised.
The classical form, as written, loses orthogonality in floating point when the
vectors are nearly dependent, so \texttt{Orthogonalize} also takes
\texttt{Method -> "ModifiedGramSchmidt"}, \texttt{"Householder"}, and
\texttt{"Reorthogonalization"}. The Kronecker product, for its part, is how
multidimensional operators are assembled from one-dimensional ones:
\S\ref{sec:linalg-sparse} builds the two-dimensional Laplacian as
\(T\otimes I + I\otimes T\).

\subsection{Determinant, inverse, and matrix powers}
\label{ssec:la-det}

The determinant collapses a square matrix to a single number that vanishes exactly
when the matrix is singular; the inverse undoes the linear map when it does not.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{linear-algebra/det/1}{\B{Det} of a numeric matrix is exact: \(1\cdot4 -
2\cdot3 = -2\).}
\pair{linear-algebra/det/2}{For a symbolic matrix it returns the determinant
multiplied out: the six signed products \(\pm a_{1\sigma(1)}a_{2\sigma(2)}a_{3\sigma(3)}\),
one for each permutation \(\sigma\) of three columns (the Leibniz formula).}
\pair{linear-algebra/det/3}{\B{Inverse} of an integer matrix comes back with exact
rational entries.}
\pair{linear-algebra/det/4}{Symbolically, the \(2\times2\) inverse is the classical
adjugate over the determinant \(ad-bc\).}
\pair{linear-algebra/det/5}{Store a matrix to check the defining identity.}
\pair{linear-algebra/det/6}{The product \(A\,A^{-1}\) is the identity, exactly.}
\pair{linear-algebra/det/7}{\B{MatrixPower} raises a matrix to an integer power by
repeated squaring; the tenth power of \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\)
is a block of consecutive Fibonacci numbers.}
\pair{linear-algebra/det/8}{It works symbolically too---here the square of a
general \(2\times2\) matrix.}
\end{codepairs}

Pair~7 is a small gem: the powers of the matrix \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\)
generate the Fibonacci sequence, because that matrix encodes the recurrence
\(F_{n+1}=F_n+F_{n-1}\). Its tenth power holds \(F_{11}=89\) and \(F_{10}=55\), and
\B{MatrixPower} reaches them in \(\lceil\log_2 10\rceil=4\) matrix multiplications
rather than nine, by repeated squaring.

The determinant and the inverse are both built from \emph{minors}\index{minor (of a
matrix)}, the determinants of square submatrices. The \emph{adjugate}
\(\operatorname{adj}A\)\index{adjugate} is the transposed matrix of signed
\((n-1)\times(n-1)\) minors, and it satisfies
\(A\,\operatorname{adj}A = (\det A)\,I\). So \(A^{-1}=\operatorname{adj}A/\det A\)
when \(\det A\neq0\), but the adjugate itself exists for every square matrix.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{linear-algebra/matfunc/1}{\texttt{Minors}\index{Minors@\mcode{Minors}} lists the \(2\times2\) minors. Entry
\((i,j)\) keeps the \(i\)-th pair of rows and the \(j\)-th pair of columns, pairs in
lexicographic order \(\{1,2\},\{1,3\},\{2,3\}\): the corner \(-3\) is
\(1\cdot5-2\cdot4\).}
\pair{linear-algebra/matfunc/2}{\texttt{Adjugate}\index{Adjugate@\mcode{Adjugate}} of the general \(2\times2\): swap the
diagonal, negate the off-diagonal. Divide by \(ad-bc\) and you have the symbolic
inverse above.}
\pair{linear-algebra/matfunc/3}{A singular matrix has no inverse but still has an
adjugate.}
\end{codepairs}

\calloutnote{Under the hood}{\B{Det} and \B{Inverse} each choose a path by the kind
of entries they see. An all-integer or rational matrix is handed to FLINT's
\texttt{fmpq\_mat\_det} / \texttt{fmpq\_mat\_inv}, which work in polynomial time and
return the exact answer---so a \(12\times12\) Hilbert determinant is instant, not an
\(O(n!)\) Laplace expansion. A machine-real matrix factorises through LAPACK's
\texttt{dgetrf} LU; a genuinely arbitrary-precision (MPFR) matrix uses an
\(O(n^3)\) MPFR LU. Only a \emph{symbolic} matrix falls to cofactor expansion,
and the cofactor form is then expanded, so pair~2 shows the flat sum of
products rather than the nested expansion along the first row. (A matrix with a
sum in a denominator keeps the cofactor form, because expanding a rational-function
determinant can blow up.) The determinant of a large machine matrix is the
product of the LU pivots and can exceed the IEEE-double range---a
\(200\times200\) random matrix has \(\lvert\det\rvert\approx10^{340}\)---so Mathilda
re-accumulates the pivot product in an MPFR value of unbounded exponent rather than
returning \texttt{inf} (\texttt{src/linalg/det.c}, \texttt{inv.c}, \texttt{matpow.c}).}

\subsection{Functions of a matrix}
\label{ssec:la-matfunc}

An integer power of a matrix needs only multiplication. To give \(e^{A}\),
\(\log A\), or \(\sqrt{A}\) a meaning, extend the function through the eigenvalues. If
\(A = S\,\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)\,S^{-1}\) then
\(f(A) = S\,\mathrm{diag}(f(\lambda_1),\dots,f(\lambda_n))\,S^{-1}\), and for a
power series such as \(e^{x}=\sum x^k/k!\) this agrees with substituting \(A\) into
the series.\index{matrix function} \texttt{MatrixExp}\index{MatrixExp@\mcode{MatrixExp}}, \texttt{MatrixLog}\index{MatrixLog@\mcode{MatrixLog}}, and the general
\texttt{MatrixFunction}\index{MatrixFunction@\mcode{MatrixFunction}} compute these, and \B{MatrixPower} accepts any exponent, not
only an integer.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{linear-algebra/matfunc/4}{The exponential of the rotation generator is a
rotation by one radian. The matrix is real with eigenvalues \(\pm i\), and the
answer is still real, in \(\cos\) and \(\sin\).}
\pair{linear-algebra/matfunc/5}{A nilpotent matrix \(N\) has \(N^2=0\), so the series
stops: \(e^{N}=I+N\).}
\pair{linear-algebra/matfunc/6}{\texttt{MatrixLog} inverts it: \(\log(I+N) = N - N^2/2 +
\cdots = N\).}
\pair{linear-algebra/matfunc/7}{On a Jordan block an undefined \(f\) shows the
general rule: \(f\) on the diagonal and \(f'\) on the superdiagonal.}
\pair{linear-algebra/matfunc/8}{A rational exponent is a matrix root. The
eigenvalues \(9\) and \(1\) become \(3\) and \(1\), and the principal square root
is again an integer matrix: \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)^2
= \left(\begin{smallmatrix}5&4\\4&5\end{smallmatrix}\right)\).}
\pair{linear-algebra/matfunc/9}{A machine exponent gives a machine answer.}
\pair{linear-algebra/matfunc/10}{And a real-exponent \B{MatrixPower} compiles, as do
\texttt{MatrixExp} and \texttt{MatrixLog}.}
\end{codepairs}

Pair~7 is why a defective matrix needs more than its eigenvalues. A Jordan block
\(\lambda I + N\) is not diagonalizable, and Taylor's theorem about \(\lambda\) gives
\(f(\lambda I+N) = f(\lambda)I + f'(\lambda)N + \tfrac12 f''(\lambda)N^2+\cdots\),
which stops once \(N^k=0\). So an exact or symbolic matrix goes through
\B{JordanDecomposition} (\S\ref{ssec:la-eigen}) and applies \(f\) block by block.
That is exact, but it is the wrong method for floating point, where the Jordan
form is not even continuous in the entries.

\calloutnote{Under the hood}{The numerical methods are the standard modern
ones. \texttt{MatrixExp} of a machine matrix uses
scaling and squaring with a Pad\'e approximant of degree \(3\) to \(13\), chosen
by the Al-Mohy--Higham backward-error bounds: \(e^{A} = (e^{A/2^s})^{2^s}\), with
\(s\) just large enough that the Pad\'e error is below rounding. An MPFR matrix uses
Taylor scaling and squaring with guard bits. \texttt{MatrixLog} runs inverse scaling and
squaring on the Schur form (MPFR: Denman--Beavers square roots), and
\texttt{MatrixFunction} uses the Schur--Parlett algorithm, taking the derivatives it needs
from \B{D}, with Bj\"orck--Hammarling for square roots. \texttt{MatrixExp[m, v]}
computes the \emph{action} \(e^{A}v\) by a truncated Taylor series without ever
forming \(e^{A}\), which is what a time-stepping code wants. On a
\B{SparseArray} it runs on sparse matrix--vector products (the \(90000\times90000\)
Laplacian in \(76\)~ms). \texttt{MatrixFunction} itself does not compile: its first
argument is a function, not a number (\texttt{src/linalg/matfunc*.c}).}

\subsection{Solving linear systems}
\label{ssec:la-linsolve}

To solve \(A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}\) is the central task of the subject. \B{LinearSolve}
does it exactly when it can, over- and under-determined systems included; when no
exact solution exists, \B{LeastSquares} returns the closest thing.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{linear-algebra/linsolve/1}{A square system, solved exactly over the
rationals.}
\pair{linear-algebra/linsolve/2}{With symbolic entries the answer is Cramer's
rule: each component is a ratio of determinants sharing the denominator
\(ru-st=\det A\).}
\pair{linear-algebra/linsolve/3}{An \emph{under}-determined system (more unknowns
than equations) returns one particular solution, with the free variables set to
zero---use \B{Solve} for the full parametrised family.}
\pair{linear-algebra/linsolve/4}{When the system is \emph{over}-determined and
inconsistent, \B{LeastSquares} minimises \(\lVert A\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\rVert\):
the best-fit line \(y = \tfrac{19}{3} + \tfrac12 x\) through \((1,7),(2,7),(3,8)\).}
\pair{linear-algebra/linsolve/5}{And when the system \emph{is} consistent, the
least-squares solution coincides with the exact one.}
\end{codepairs}

The distinction in pairs~3 and~4 is the whole geometry of the problem.
\B{LinearSolve} answers ``is \(\mathbf{b}\) in the column
space\index{Cramer's rule} of \(A\)?''---if so,
which combination of columns produces it. When \(A\) has more columns than rows the
answer is a whole affine subspace, and \B{LinearSolve} hands back one point of it;
when \(A\) has more rows than columns and \(\mathbf{b}\) lies off the column space,
there is no solution at all, and \B{LeastSquares} instead returns the
\(\mathbf{x}\) whose image \(A\mathbf{x}\) is the orthogonal projection of
\(\mathbf{b}\) onto the column space---the least-squares fit\index{least squares} that underlies all of
linear regression (and \B{Fit}, in \S\ref{sec:statistics}).

\calloutnote{Theory}{\B{LeastSquares} minimises \(\lVert A\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\rVert_2\).
Setting the gradient to zero gives the \emph{normal equations} \(A^{*}A\,\mathbf{x} =
A^{*}\mathbf{b}\), whose solution is \(\mathbf{x}=A^{+}\mathbf{b}\) with \(A^{+}\) the
\B{PseudoInverse}\index{Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse}. For a rank-deficient \(A\)
the minimiser is not unique, and \(A^{+}\mathbf{b}\) picks out the one of smallest
norm. A machine-real system takes a thin SVD rather than forming \(A^{+}\)
explicitly---a \(500\times500\) least-squares fit that never finished on the exact
pipeline costs \(77\)~ms this way (\texttt{src/linalg/lstsq.c}).}

\subsection{Rank, row reduction, and the null space}
\label{ssec:la-reduction}

Beneath solving lies elimination. \B{RowReduce} brings a matrix to reduced row
echelon form; \B{MatrixRank} counts the independent rows; and \B{NullSpace}
returns a basis for the vectors the matrix annihilates. The three are one
computation seen from three angles.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{linear-algebra/reduction/1}{\B{RowReduce} of a rank-deficient matrix: the
bottom row of zeros is the dependency made visible.}
\pair{linear-algebra/reduction/2}{\B{MatrixRank} reports \(2\)---the third row is
the sum of a scaling of the first two.}
\pair{linear-algebra/reduction/3}{\B{NullSpace} returns the one vector \(\{1,-2,1\}\)
spanning the kernel.}
\pair{linear-algebra/reduction/4}{Store the matrix.}
\pair{linear-algebra/reduction/5}{Confirm it: \(A\) times the null
vector is \(\mathbf 0\).}
\pair{linear-algebra/reduction/6}{These are exact even symbolically---row-reducing
a matrix whose third row is \(\text{row}_1+\text{row}_2\) exposes the zero row and
the exact rational entries above.}
\pair{linear-algebra/reduction/7}{\B{MatrixRank} sees the symbolic dependency too:
the second row is \(2\) times the first, so the rank is \(1\).}
\end{codepairs}

The rank-nullity theorem\index{rank-nullity theorem} is on display: a
\(3\times3\) matrix of rank \(2\)
(pair~2) has a null space of dimension \(3-2=1\) (pair~3), and their sum recovers
the column count. That Mathilda finds these exactly, over rationals \emph{and} over
symbols, is the point of pairs~6 and~7---the elimination is fraction-free, so no
spurious denominator ever appears.

\calloutnote{Under the hood}{The default reduction is a Bareiss-like
\emph{fraction-free} Gauss--Jordan elimination\index{Bareiss algorithm}: it clears
each column with cross-multiplications chosen so that the exact divisions leave no
denominator larger than necessary, keeping intermediate coefficients from
exploding the way naive Euclidean elimination would. An all-integer or rational
matrix instead goes to FLINT's \texttt{fmpq\_mat\_rref}, whose reduced echelon form
is unique and therefore matches. \B{MatrixRank} and \B{NullSpace} both call back
into \B{RowReduce} through the evaluator, so every improvement to it propagates to
them for free (\texttt{src/linalg/linsolve.c}, \texttt{matrank.c},
\texttt{nullspace.c}).}

The entries need not be rational functions of the symbols. Radicals of the
parameters are allowed too, and the elimination still decides every pivot
exactly.\index{row reduction!over radicals}

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{linear-algebra/radicals/1}{The second row is \(\sqrt{c}\) times the first,
but only if you know that \(\sqrt{c}\cdot c = c^{3/2}\).}
\pair{linear-algebra/radicals/2}{\B{MatrixRank} knows it: rank \(2\).}
\pair{linear-algebra/radicals/3}{So the null space is one-dimensional, with
\(\sqrt{c}\) in its denominators.}
\pair{linear-algebra/radicals/4}{Check it: \(m\) times the vector is zero once
\B{Together} combines the fractions.}
\pair{linear-algebra/radicals/5}{\B{LinearSolve} over the same field
\(\mathbb{Q}(c)(\sqrt{c})\).}
\end{codepairs}

\calloutnote{Under the hood}{The fraction-free elimination divides each entry
exactly by the previous pivot. That division used to treat \(c\), \(\sqrt{c}\), and
\(c^{3/2}\) as three unrelated variables, so a quotient such as
\((5440a^3c^2+\cdots)/(-10\sqrt{c})\) was judged inexact. The leftover fraction then
hid a zero pivot, and an \(11\times8\) system of rank \(6\) came back with rank
\(8\) and an empty null space. Now each radical base \(B\) with root index \(m\) is
renamed \(g^m\) for a fresh generator \(g\). The division is done in the polynomial
ring in \(g\), the result is mapped back by \(g\mapsto B^{1/m}\), and it is checked
in the original ring. For larger systems over \(\mathbb{Q}(\text{params})(\text{radicals})\),
\B{RowReduce} maps the whole field onto a purely transcendental one and runs a
fraction-free Gauss--Jordan in FLINT's multivariate polynomials. A \(17\times6\)
system whose entries had some \(200\) terms each never finished before. It now
reduces in a second. Radicals of numbers, such as \(\sqrt2\), are left out of the
renaming, because \(\sqrt2\) treated as a free \(g\) would forget that \(g^2=2\)
(\texttt{src/linalg/util.c}, \texttt{src/poly/flint\_bridge.c}). Systems like these
arise as the ansatz solves of symbolic integration.}

\subsection{Exact reduction and the \texttt{ZeroTest} option}
\label{ssec:la-zerotest}

Elimination lives or dies on one question asked at every pivot: \emph{is this entry
zero?} For numbers it is trivial. For symbols it is the hardest question in the
system, and it is where exact linear algebra quietly goes wrong if you are not
careful. Consider a matrix whose two rows are equal---but equal only after a
trigonometric identity that Mathilda does not apply on its own.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{linear-algebra/zerotest/1}{The Pythagorean identity is true, but it does not
auto-simplify: Mathilda leaves \texttt{Cos[x]\^{}2 + Sin[x]\^{}2} as written.}
\pair{linear-algebra/zerotest/2}{So these two rows are mathematically identical---the
top-left entry \emph{is} \(1\)---but not \emph{structurally} identical.}
\pair{linear-algebra/zerotest/3}{The default \B{NullSpace} uses a structural zero
test. It cannot see that the rows are dependent, so it wrongly reports full rank
and an empty kernel.}
\pair{linear-algebra/zerotest/4}{Supply \texttt{ZeroTest -> (Simplify[\#] === 0 \&)}
and the reduction consults \B{Simplify} at every pivot---now it finds the
dependency and returns the true null vector.}
\end{codepairs}

This is the reason for the user-facing \texttt{ZeroTest} option on \B{RowReduce}
and \B{NullSpace}.\index{ZeroTest option}\index{zero testing!symbolic} The default
structural test treats each distinct radical or transcendental as an independent
symbol---fast, and correct whenever the answer is structurally apparent, but blind
to any zero that only appears after simplification. Passing a predicate---a body
like \texttt{(Simplify[\#] === 0 \&)} or a head like \B{PossibleZeroQ}---replaces
that test with a semantic one, and the elimination pivots on genuine, not merely
apparent, non-zeros. There is no \emph{perfect} zero test---that this must be so is a
theorem, not a shortfall of effort---so how hard to test at each pivot is a choice left to
you; \S\ref{ssec:alg-possiblezeroq} takes up \mcode{PossibleZeroQ} and the undecidability
theorem behind it.

The same fault line runs through \emph{numeric} reduction, where the question
becomes ``is this entry small enough to treat as zero?'' Here the answer depends on
a tolerance, and the exact and machine paths part company sharply.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{linear-algebra/zerotest/5}{A matrix whose two off-diagonal pivots are tiny
but exactly non-zero.}
\pair{linear-algebra/zerotest/6}{Exactly, this matrix has three independent
rows---the tiny entries \(10^{-10}\) and \(10^{-20}\) are genuinely non-zero.}
\pair{linear-algebra/zerotest/7}{Convert to machine reals and one pivot falls below
the default tolerance: \B{MatrixRank} now reports \(2\).}
\pair{linear-algebra/zerotest/8}{Force \texttt{Tolerance -> 0} and every non-zero
counts again, recovering the rank of \(3\).}
\end{codepairs}

Neither answer is wrong; they answer different questions. Exact reduction asks
whether an entry is \emph{algebraically} zero and keeps the tiny pivots; machine
reduction asks whether it is zero \emph{to working precision} and discards them,
which is exactly what you want when the small entries are round-off rather than
signal. The lesson is to know which question you are asking---and to reach for
\texttt{Tolerance} (numeric) or \texttt{ZeroTest} (exact) when the default answers
a different one.

\subsection{Matrix decompositions}
\label{ssec:la-decomp}

A decomposition rewrites a matrix as a product of simpler factors---triangular,
orthogonal, diagonal---and almost every serious numerical algorithm is a
decomposition in disguise. We start with the classical four.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{linear-algebra/decomp/1}{\B{LUDecomposition} returns the combined
lower/upper factors, the pivot permutation, and a condition estimate---here the
factorisation of a Vandermonde-like matrix.}
\pair{linear-algebra/decomp/2}{\B{QRDecomposition} factors \(A\) into an
orthonormal \texttt{q} and an upper-triangular \texttt{r}. This is the classic
textbook \(3\times3\), and its \(Q\) is exactly rational.}
\pair{linear-algebra/decomp/3}{The orthonormal factor \texttt{q}: its rows are
mutually perpendicular unit vectors.}
\pair{linear-algebra/decomp/4}{The upper-triangular factor \texttt{r}.}
\pair{linear-algebra/decomp/5}{And they reconstruct the original exactly:
\(Q^{\mathsf T} R = A\).}
\pair{linear-algebra/decomp/6}{\B{SingularValueDecomposition} of a rank-\(1\)
matrix: a single non-zero singular value \(\sqrt{10}\) on the diagonal of
\(\Sigma\), the rest zero.}
\pair{linear-algebra/decomp/7}{A symmetric machine matrix for the Schur form.}
\pair{linear-algebra/decomp/8}{It is factored by \B{SchurDecomposition}.}
\pair{linear-algebra/decomp/9}{The result is an orthonormal \texttt{q} and an
upper-triangular \texttt{t} (diagonal here, since the matrix is symmetric): its
diagonal carries the eigenvalues.}
\pair{linear-algebra/decomp/10}{\B{Chop} confirms \(A = Q\,T\,Q^{*}\) to machine
zero.}
\end{codepairs}

Each factorisation answers a different need. \B{LUDecomposition} is the engine of
\B{LinearSolve}: once \(A=LU\), each right-hand side costs only two triangular
solves. \B{QRDecomposition} orthogonalises the columns and is the stable way to
solve least-squares problems and the workhorse of the eigenvalue algorithm.
\B{SingularValueDecomposition} is the most revealing of all---the singular values
are the semi-axes of the ellipsoid \(A\) maps the unit sphere onto, the number of
non-zero ones is the rank (pair~6 makes a rank-\(1\) matrix show a single
\(\sqrt{10}\)), and truncating the small ones is the mathematics of data
compression and \B{PseudoInverse}. \B{SchurDecomposition}\index{Schur decomposition} is the theoretical keystone:
\emph{every} square matrix is unitarily
similar to a (quasi-)upper-triangular one whose diagonal is the spectrum, so the
eigenvalues can be read off without ever solving a polynomial.

\calloutnote{Under the hood}{The decompositions dispatch on precision. A
machine-real matrix goes to LAPACK---\texttt{dgetrf} (LU), a Householder
\texttt{dgeqrf} (QR)\index{Householder reflection}, divide-and-conquer
\texttt{dgesdd} (SVD), and the Francis QR iteration \texttt{dgees} (Schur)---wired
through a four-tier autodetection ladder (Apple Accelerate, then two flavours of
system LAPACK, then a graceful fallback). An arbitrary-precision matrix runs a
hand-rolled MPFR kernel at the requested precision (Householder QR, one-sided
Jacobi SVD, Hessenberg+Francis Schur). An exact or symbolic matrix stays on a
modified Gram--Schmidt or eigendecomposition path and returns exact
\texttt{Sqrt[\ldots]} forms. \B{SchurDecomposition} is numeric only---a generic
matrix has no closed-form symbolic Schur form---and is left unevaluated on symbolic
input rather than answered wrongly (\texttt{src/linalg/ludecomp.c},
\texttt{qrdecomp.c}, \texttt{svdecomp.c}, \texttt{schurdecomp.c},
\texttt{lapack.c}).}

Several more factorisations serve special purposes. A Hermitian positive-definite
matrix factors as \(A = C^{*}C\) with \(C\) upper triangular, the \emph{Cholesky
decomposition}\index{Cholesky decomposition}. It costs half an LU and needs no
pivoting. The \emph{Hessenberg form}\index{Hessenberg form} is zero below the first
subdiagonal and is reached by a finite sequence of unitary similarities; it is the
first stage of every practical eigenvalue algorithm. \texttt{SingularValueList}\index{SingularValueList@\mcode{SingularValueList}} gives the
singular values without the singular vectors, and \texttt{LUBackSubstitution}\index{LUBackSubstitution@\mcode{LUBackSubstitution}} reuses an
\B{LUDecomposition} for new right-hand sides.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{linear-algebra/decomp2/1}{\texttt{CholeskyDecomposition}\index{CholeskyDecomposition@\mcode{CholeskyDecomposition}} of an exact matrix is
exact: \(C\) is upper triangular with a \(\sqrt2\) in the corner.}
\pair{linear-algebra/decomp2/2}{And \(C^{*}C\) gives the matrix back.}
\pair{linear-algebra/decomp2/3}{This matrix is symmetric but indefinite (its
eigenvalues are \(3\) and \(-1\)), so it has no Cholesky factor. The call stays
unevaluated, and at the prompt it also prints
\texttt{CholeskyDecomposition::npdef}.}
\pair{linear-algebra/decomp2/4}{\texttt{HessenbergDecomposition}\index{HessenbergDecomposition@\mcode{HessenbergDecomposition}} gives a unitary
\texttt{p} and the Hessenberg \texttt{h}.}
\pair{linear-algebra/decomp2/5}{\texttt{h} has one zero, below the subdiagonal---for
a \(3\times3\) that is all the structure there is.}
\pair{linear-algebra/decomp2/6}{The similarity \(A = P\,H\,P^{*}\) holds to rounding.}
\pair{linear-algebra/decomp2/7}{Exact singular values are square roots of the
eigenvalues of \(A^{*}A\), here nested radicals.}
\pair{linear-algebra/decomp2/8}{A rank-\(1\) machine matrix has one nonzero singular
value. \texttt{SingularValueList} drops the values below its tolerance, so the list has
length \(1\), and its length is the numerical rank.}
\pair{linear-algebra/decomp2/9}{Factor once.}
\pair{linear-algebra/decomp2/10}{Then each right-hand side is two triangular solves.
The answer matches \texttt{LinearSolve[\{\{1, 2\}, \{3, 4\}\}, \{5, 6\}]}.}
\pair{linear-algebra/decomp2/11}{A matrix right-hand side solves for every column;
with the identity, that is the inverse.}
\end{codepairs}

\texttt{HessenbergDecomposition} is numerical only, as \B{SchurDecomposition} is: an
exact matrix gives \texttt{HessenbergDecomposition::schurf} and is left
unevaluated. Its machine factors come from LAPACK's \texttt{dgehrd} and
\texttt{dorghr}, and its MPFR factors follow LAPACK's reflector convention, so the two
agree to machine precision. Cholesky, the singular-value list, and the Hessenberg
form all lower inside \B{Compile}, each on the same machine kernel as the
interpreter, so the compiled and interpreted answers agree entry for entry
(\texttt{src/linalg/cholesky.c}, \texttt{lapack.c}).

\subsection{Eigenvalues, eigenvectors, and the Jordan form}
\label{ssec:la-eigen}

An eigenvector is a direction the matrix merely scales; its eigenvalue is the
scale. Finding them is the deepest structural question you can ask of a linear map,
and it is where the exact/numeric split is most vivid.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{linear-algebra/eigen/1}{\B{CharacteristicPolynomial} gives \(\det(A - xI)\),
whose roots are the eigenvalues: \(x^2 - 5x - 2\) here.}
\pair{linear-algebra/eigen/2}{\B{Eigenvalues} of an exact matrix stays exact,
returning genuine surds---and a \(0\), certifying the matrix is singular.}
\pair{linear-algebra/eigen/3}{Symbolically, the \(2\times2\) eigenvalues are the
trace-and-determinant quadratic formula. With free symbols there is no modulus to
sort by, so the two roots come in the order \B{Solve} returns them: here the
minus root first.}
\pair{linear-algebra/eigen/4}{A \(90^\circ\) rotation has no real eigenvectors, so
its eigenvalues are the complex pair \(\pm i\).}
\pair{linear-algebra/eigen/5}{\B{Eigenvectors} of a defective matrix: the repeated
eigenvalue \(2\) supplies only one eigenvector, so the deficient slot is padded
with a zero vector to keep the list aligned with the eigenvalues.}
\end{codepairs}

Pair~5 is the crack through which the Jordan form enters. The matrix there has
eigenvalue \(2\) with algebraic multiplicity \(2\) but only \emph{one} independent
eigenvector---it is \emph{defective}, not diagonalizable. The zero-vector padding
is Mathilda telling you honestly that an eigenvector is missing. The remedy is the
\emph{Jordan canonical form}\index{Jordan canonical form}: the closest a defective
matrix can come to diagonal, with the missing eigenvectors replaced by
\emph{generalized} ones\index{generalized eigenvector} and a tell-tale \(1\) on the
superdiagonal.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{linear-algebra/eigen/6}{A matrix with a defective eigenvalue \(12\).}
\pair{linear-algebra/eigen/7}{\B{JordanDecomposition} returns the similarity
\(S\) and the Jordan form \(J\).}
\pair{linear-algebra/eigen/8}{The form \(J\) has a \(2\times2\) Jordan block for
\(12\): the eigenvalue twice on the diagonal, a \(1\) above it marking the deficient
eigenspace.}
\pair{linear-algebra/eigen/9}{And the decomposition reconstructs the matrix:
\(A = S\,J\,S^{-1}\).}
\end{codepairs}

The superdiagonal \(1\) in pair~8 is the entire content of the Jordan form: it says
that eigenvalue \(12\) has a two-step chain of generalized eigenvectors rather than
two independent ones. Read \(J\) and you read the matrix's deepest invariant---the
sizes of its Jordan blocks---which no similarity transformation can change.

\calloutnote{Under the hood}{\B{Eigenvalues} does \emph{not} form \(\det(A-xI)\) and
expand a symbolic determinant; it uses the Faddeev--LeVerrier--Souriau
recurrence\index{Faddeev-LeVerrier algorithm}, which produces the characteristic
polynomial in \(O(n^4)\) matrix multiplications, then hands the polynomial to
\B{Solve}---so eigenvalues of a large exact matrix return quickly, and the exact
roots come back as radicals or \B{Root} objects (\S\ref{sec:algebra}). The exact
\B{JordanDecomposition} reads each block's size from the nullity sequence
\(\dim\ker(A-\lambda I)^k\); a machine-real matrix with a distinct spectrum is
diagonalizable and takes a direct LAPACK \texttt{dgeev} path (a \(100\times100\)
in \(\approx\!9.6\)~ms), while a defective or ambiguous numeric matrix is
rationalised, decomposed exactly, and numericalised back to recover the true block
structure (\texttt{src/linalg/eigen.c}, \texttt{charpoly.c}, \texttt{jordandecomp.c}).}

\subsection{The machine--exact divide}
\label{ssec:la-precision}

Every routine in this section has, in fact, been choosing between worlds all along.
It is worth watching the choice happen on one matrix. The Hilbert matrix---entries
\(1/(i+j-1)\), symmetric and famously ill-conditioned---is the perfect specimen.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{linear-algebra/precision/1}{Exact: \B{Det} of the \(5\times5\)
\B{HilbertMatrix} is the precise rational \(1/266716800000\).}
\pair{linear-algebra/precision/2}{Machine: the same determinant over doubles is a
single \texttt{Real}---accurate to the digits shown, but a rounded number, not an
exact one. (\texttt{3.7493*\^{}-12} is the input-form spelling of
\(3.7493\times10^{-12}\).)}
\pair{linear-algebra/precision/3}{Arbitrary precision: at \(40\) digits (MPFR) the
determinant of the \(7\times7\) Hilbert matrix comes back to full requested
precision.}
\pair{linear-algebra/precision/4}{Exact inversion of an ill-conditioned matrix is
no trouble at all when the arithmetic is exact---integer entries, computed by
FLINT.}
\pair{linear-algebra/precision/5}{The visible packed form: an \B{NDArray} is a
dense machine buffer that announces itself.}
\pair{linear-algebra/precision/6}{\B{Dot} over two \texttt{NDArray}s runs on the raw
buffers and returns a closed-system \texttt{NDArray}.}
\pair{linear-algebra/precision/7}{\B{Det} of one returns a bare machine
scalar, straight off the buffer.}
\end{codepairs}

The three determinants---an exact rational, a machine \texttt{Real}, and a
\(40\)-digit MPFR number---are the same mathematical quantity computed in three
number systems, and Mathilda selects among them purely from the \emph{kind} of the
entries. Exact in, exact out (via FLINT or fraction-free elimination); machine in,
LAPACK out; high-precision MPFR in, an MPFR kernel out. Nothing about the syntax
changes; the representation of the data decides the path.\index{arbitrary precision}\index{LAPACK}

\calloutnote{Performance}{The packed array is what makes the machine path fast, and
the rule that keeps it safe is the \emph{transparency gate}\index{packed array!transparency gate}: a
machine-number list may be held as a raw buffer, and a numeric routine on the
``aware'' list (\B{Dot}, \B{Det}, \B{Inverse}, \B{LinearSolve}, \B{Norm},
\B{MatrixRank}, and their kin) reads that buffer directly---no per-element boxing,
no re-parsing---so a \(300\times300\) \texttt{MatrixPower[A, 4]} is the \(0.69\)~ms of
its two \texttt{dgemm} calls. A routine \emph{not} on that list transparently
un-packs first, trading speed for correctness but never the answer. The same
machine heads lower inside \B{Compile}, so a linear-algebra kernel written at the
prompt runs at full speed inside a compiled numerical loop
(\texttt{src/pack.c}, \texttt{src/linalg/ndlinalg.c}; see also
\S\ref{sec:statistics} on packed reductions).}

The third world, arbitrary precision, is the one to reach for when a matrix is
too ill-conditioned for doubles but too large or too irrational for exact
arithmetic.\index{arbitrary precision!linear algebra} A condition number
\(\kappa\) costs about \(\log_{10}\kappa\) digits, so a machine computation with
\(\kappa\approx10^{16}\) has nothing left. Give the same computation \(100\) digits
and it keeps more than eighty.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{linear-algebra/mpfr/1}{The \(40\times40\) Hilbert matrix is invertible, but at
machine precision \B{MatrixRank} sees only \(21\) directions above rounding level.}
\pair{linear-algebra/mpfr/2}{At \(100\) digits the elimination runs at that precision,
and all \(40\) pivots are resolved.}
\pair{linear-algebra/mpfr/3}{Solving \(H_{12}\mathbf x=\mathbf 1\) at \(40\) digits.
The exact first component is \(-12\); \(\kappa(H_{12})\approx1.7\times10^{16}\), and
the last ten digits printed are rounding error, amplified.}
\pair{linear-algebra/mpfr/4}{Eigenvalues of a \emph{nonsymmetric} matrix at
\(30\) digits. Their sum is the trace, \(16\).}
\pair{linear-algebra/mpfr/5}{A generalized eigenproblem \(A\mathbf x=\lambda B\mathbf x\),
given as the pencil \(\{A, B\}\): \(\det(A-\lambda B) = (2\lambda-5)(\lambda-1)\).}
\pair{linear-algebra/mpfr/6}{Singular values at \(30\) digits, spanning four orders
of magnitude.}
\end{codepairs}

\calloutnote{Pitfall}{Pair~3 prints forty digits, but only about thirty are right.
Mathilda's arbitrary-precision numbers carry a fixed working precision, not an
error estimate, so a result is printed to the precision it was computed at, however
much the conditioning has eaten. When the answer matters, estimate the condition
number, or compute at two precisions and keep the digits on which they agree.}

\calloutnote{Under the hood}{An MPFR matrix used to be multiplied the way a symbolic
one is, with an evaluator \B{Times} and \B{Plus} for every term. Now each output entry
of \B{Dot} is one \emph{fused} dot product\index{fused dot product}
(\texttt{arb\_approx\_dot}), a single library call instead of \(n\) multiplications
and \(n-1\) additions, and the rows are spread over threads: a \(256\times256\)
matrix times a vector at \(800\) digits went from \(0.35\)~s to \(0.02\)~s. The MPFR
\B{LUDecomposition}, and the square \B{LinearSolve} built on it, compute each
entry the same way (about \(4.5\times\) faster), and \B{LinearSolve} judges a pivot
zero relative to the working precision. Until that change both \B{MatrixRank} and the
singularity test of \B{LinearSolve} rounded MPFR entries to doubles:
\texttt{MatrixRank[N[HilbertMatrix[40], 100]]} said \(21\), the machine answer of
pair~1. A nonsymmetric \B{Eigenvalues} reduces to
Hessenberg form with fused products and runs Francis QR sweeps on the active block
only. A pencil \(\{A, B\}\) with symmetric \(A\) and positive-definite \(B\) is
reduced by a Cholesky factor of \(B\), so its eigenvalues are real by construction.
Before that, an MPFR pencil was silently computed in doubles. \texttt{SingularValueList}
uses Householder bidiagonalisation followed by the Golub--Kahan implicit-shift QR
iteration\index{Golub-Kahan SVD} at the input precision plus \(32\) bits. A
\(50\times50\) Vandermonde matrix at \(100\) digits takes \(74\)~ms where one-sided
Jacobi took \(2\)~s, and every digit agrees with a recomputation at \(200\). (The full
\B{SingularValueDecomposition} still uses Jacobi.) One benchmark is problem~1 of the
ISSAC~'97 challenge, the eigenvalues of \texttt{N[HilbertMatrix[256], 800]}, which went from
\(16\)~s to about \(6.5\)~s.}

\subsection{Sparse matrices}
\label{sec:linalg-sparse}

The matrices that arise from differential equations, networks, and finite
elements are large and almost entirely zero.\index{sparse matrix} The
second-difference matrix of a grid of \(n\) points has three nonzeros per row,
whatever \(n\) is. Storing all \(n^2\) entries wastes memory, and multiplying
by them wastes time. A \B{SparseArray} stores only the nonzero entries, with their
positions, and the linear algebra of this section works on it directly, without
ever building the dense matrix. (Its construction, storage and element-wise
arithmetic are covered with the other data structures in \S\ref{sec:ds-sparse}.)
The usual constructor is a list of rules, and \texttt{Band}\index{Band@\mcode{Band}}
fills a whole diagonal at once.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{linear-algebra/sparse/1}{The \(6\times6\) second-difference matrix: \(2\) on the
diagonal, \(-1\) on the two diagonals beside it. It prints as its count of stored
entries and its dimensions.}
\pair{linear-algebra/sparse/2}{\B{Normal} gives the dense matrix, when you want to
see it.}
\pair{linear-algebra/sparse/3}{A sparse product stays sparse: the square of a
tridiagonal matrix is pentadiagonal.}
\pair{linear-algebra/sparse/4}{Exact entries give an exact solution,
\(x_i = i(7-i)/2\), the discrete parabola.}
\pair{linear-algebra/sparse/5}{The determinant of the \(n\times n\)
second-difference matrix is \(n+1\).}
\end{codepairs}

Real problems are bigger. The two-dimensional Laplacian on a \(k\times k\) grid is
built from the one-dimensional \(T\) as \(T\otimes I + I\otimes T\), the
\texttt{KroneckerProduct} of \S\ref{ssec:la-products} applied to sparse factors.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{linear-algebra/sparse/6}{A grid of \(200\times200\) points.}
\pair{linear-algebra/sparse/7}{The one-dimensional operator, now with machine
entries.}

identity.}
\pair{linear-algebra/sparse/9}{The Laplacian is \(40000\times40000\): \(1.6\times10^9\)
entries, of which \(199200\) are stored.}
\pair{linear-algebra/sparse/10}{About one entry in eight thousand is nonzero.}
\pair{linear-algebra/sparse/11}{A right-hand side of ones.}
\pair{linear-algebra/sparse/12}{\B{LinearSolve} factors the sparse matrix directly.}
\pair{linear-algebra/sparse/13}{The residual \(\lVert L\mathbf u - \mathbf b\rVert\),
over all forty thousand equations, is at rounding level.}
\pair{linear-algebra/sparse/14}{\texttt{LinearSolve[m]} with no right-hand side
returns a \texttt{LinearSolveFunction}\index{LinearSolveFunction@\mcode{LinearSolveFunction}}, which holds the factorisation for reuse.}
\pair{linear-algebra/sparse/15}{Applying it to \(\mathbf b\) gives the same solution
again, without refactoring.}
\pair{linear-algebra/sparse/16}{An iterative method instead: conjugate gradients,

\pair{linear-algebra/sparse/17}{It agrees with the direct solve to about

\pair{linear-algebra/sparse/18}{\texttt{Eigenvalues[m, -3]} asks for the three

\pair{linear-algebra/sparse/19}{They match the closed form (see the Theory note below)

\pair{linear-algebra/sparse/20}{\texttt{SingularValueList} with a count works the same

\end{codepairs}

\calloutnote{Theory}{The one-dimensional matrix \(T\) of order \(k\) has
eigenvalues \(4\sin^2\!\bigl(j\pi/(2(k+1))\bigr)\), \(j=1,\dots,k\), with sine vectors
as eigenvectors. The eigenvalues of \(T\otimes I + I\otimes T\) are all the sums
\(\lambda_i+\lambda_j\), so the smallest is \(\lambda_1+\lambda_1\) and the next is
the double value \(\lambda_1+\lambda_2=\lambda_2+\lambda_1\). Pair~18 lists them in
order of decreasing magnitude, as \B{Eigenvalues} always does, so the double value
comes first. The smallest eigenvalue is about \(2\pi^2/k^2\), and the condition
number \(\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}\approx 4k^2/\pi^2\) grows with the grid. That
growth is why conjugate gradients needs more iterations on finer grids, and why
preconditioners exist.}

The sparse solvers are not limited to machine numbers. An exact sparse system is
eliminated exactly, and an arbitrary-precision one is solved at its own
precision.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{linear-algebra/sparse/21}{The \(1000\times1000\) second-difference matrix, exact.}
\pair{linear-algebra/sparse/22}{Its exact solution; the middle component is
\(500\cdot501/2\).}
\pair{linear-algebra/sparse/23}{The same system at \(40\) digits.}
\end{codepairs}

\B{LinearSolve} takes a \texttt{Method} option for a machine matrix, dense or sparse:
\texttt{"Multifrontal"} (sparse LU), \texttt{"Cholesky"}, or \texttt{"Krylov"}. The
Krylov method takes its own sub-options, \texttt{Method -> "ConjugateGradient"},
\texttt{"BiCGSTAB"} (the default) or \texttt{"GMRES"}, and
\texttt{"Preconditioner" -> "ILU0"} (the default), \texttt{"ILUT"} or
\texttt{"ILUTP"}, with \texttt{Tolerance}, \texttt{"MaxIterations"} and a
\texttt{"StartingVector"}.\index{Krylov methods} The eigensolvers take
\texttt{Method -> \{"Arnoldi", "Shift" -> s\}} for the eigenvalues nearest
\(s\), and \texttt{Eigenvalues}, \texttt{Eigensystem}\index{Eigensystem@\mcode{Eigensystem}}, \texttt{SingularValueList} and
\texttt{SingularValueDecomposition} all accept a count \(k\) (largest), \(-k\)
(smallest), or a pencil \(\{A, B\}\).

\calloutnote{Pitfall}{A singular \emph{dense} machine matrix gives the minimum-norm
solution of a consistent system. A singular machine \B{SparseArray} gives
\texttt{LinearSolve::sing} and stays unevaluated, even when the system is
consistent. This difference from the dense path is deliberate. If you need the minimum-norm answer, use \texttt{Normal} or \B{LeastSquares}. A head with no sparse
kernel densifies its argument, and refuses with \texttt{SparseArray::dense} beyond
\(2^{27}\) entries, so check that a very large computation stays on the sparse
path.}

\calloutnote{Under the hood}{A \B{SparseArray} is stored as compressed sparse rows
(\S\ref{sec:ds-sparse}). \B{Dot} has native kernels for
sparse--vector (SpMV), sparse--dense (SpMM) and sparse--sparse products, the last by
Gustavson's two-pass algorithm, threaded, with an exact fallback on integer
overflow. \B{MatrixPower} of a \B{SparseArray} squares sparsely. For
\B{LinearSolve}, a machine matrix up to order \(128\) is factored by a dense LAPACK
LU. Above that it goes to UMFPACK or CHOLMOD when Mathilda is built with
SuiteSparse. Without SuiteSparse, as in the build that produced this book, a
\emph{band solver} renumbers the matrix by reverse Cuthill--McKee and factors it in
LAPACK band storage (band Cholesky, else band LU), in \(O(n\,b^2)\) for bandwidth
\(b\). That takes a \(10000\times10000\) pentadiagonal system from \(5.4\)~s with the
dense LU to a few milliseconds. When no band pays, Krylov iterations take over, refined
in doubled precision until they are as accurate as a direct solve. The eigensolvers
use ARPACK when it is linked and a native Krylov--Schur (thick-restart Lanczos
for Hermitian matrices) otherwise. The \(-k\) form works by shift-and-invert about
\(0\), through the same sparse factorisation. An exact sparse system is eliminated
over FLINT rationals with Markowitz pivoting, so a fill-free system costs \(O(\mathrm{nnz})\)
rational operations. An MPFR system is factored once in machine arithmetic and then
refined, with residuals in MPFR over the nonzeros. The corrections must shrink
geometrically, and that certifies the result: \(60\) digits of a
\(20000\times20000\) tridiagonal system take \(0.08\)~s (\texttt{src/sparse/}).}

\calloutnote{Performance}{Against \texttt{scipy.sparse} (benchmarks
\texttt{120-sparse-arrays}, \texttt{124-sparse-linalg}). On a \(10^5\times10^5\)
matrix with \(10^6\) nonzeros, a hundred SpMVs take \(49\)~ms (scipy \(245\)~ms) and
\(A\cdot A\) takes \(36\)~ms (\(174\)~ms). With SuiteSparse linked, a direct solve of the
\(500\times500\)-grid Poisson problem takes \(0.70\)~s against \(10.3\)~s for scipy's
SuperLU, and GMRES with ILUT on a \(300^2\) convection--diffusion operator \(1.35\)~s
against \(20.9\)~s. The six smallest eigenvalues of the \(90000\times90000\)
Laplacian take \(2.2\)~s (scipy's shift-invert \texttt{eigsh}: \(3.4\)~s). The six
\emph{largest} eigenvalues of the same Laplacian, which take ARPACK many restarts,
cost \(69\)~s against scipy's \(64\)~s, which is parity. Mathilda is behind in one
place: unpreconditioned conjugate gradients at \(10^6\) unknowns is \(2.3\times\)
slower, because scipy's loop is BLAS calls on contiguous buffers.}

\subsection*{Where this connects}

Linear algebra is the substrate the rest of the system computes on. The
least-squares solve of \S\ref{ssec:la-linsolve} is the machinery behind \B{Fit}
and the correlation matrix of \S\ref{sec:statistics}, both of them a \B{Dot} of a
matrix with its \B{Transpose}. The exact eigenvalues of \S\ref{ssec:la-eigen} flow
straight out of the polynomial solving and \B{Root} objects of \S\ref{sec:algebra},
and the fraction-free elimination beneath \B{RowReduce} is the same idea as the
subresultant sequences that tame coefficient growth there. The machine kernels and
the packed substrate reappear wherever numbers are crunched in bulk---the numerical
calculus, the optimizers, the machine-learning primitives---and every machine head
named here lowers inside \B{Compile} (Chapter~\ref{ch:compilation}), so the
linear algebra you write interactively is the linear algebra that runs in a tight
loop. The sparse solvers and eigensolvers of \S\ref{sec:linalg-sparse} are what a
discretised differential equation or a large graph needs: the same
\texttt{LinearSolve} and \texttt{Eigenvalues}, on matrices far beyond what a dense
array could hold. For the exhaustive options of any function above, its reference page is one
\texttt{?Name} away at the prompt.
