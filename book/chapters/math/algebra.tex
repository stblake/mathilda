\section{Algebra}
\label{sec:algebra}

Arithmetic computes with numbers; algebra computes with the \emph{polynomials} and
\emph{rational functions}\index{rational function} you build when you let symbols stand
in for numbers. These are the central objects of this section, and everything the
previous section established about exactness carries straight over: a polynomial with
rational coefficients keeps those coefficients exact, a factorization is a genuine
identity and not an approximation, and a solution is returned as the algebraic number it
is rather than a decimal that happens to be close.

A polynomial in Mathilda is not a special kind of value. It is an ordinary
expression---a tree of \B{Plus}, \B{Times}, and \B{Power}---and the algebra functions
read its structure off that tree.\calloutnote{Under the hood}{There is no dedicated
polynomial data type: \texttt{x\textasciicircum 2 + 3 x + 2} is the same
\texttt{Plus[\ldots]} expression the evaluator manipulates everywhere else. When an
operation is heavy---expanding a large power, factoring, a polynomial GCD---Mathilda
copies the relevant part into a packed FLINT~\cite{flint} \texttt{fmpq\_mpoly}
(a multivariate polynomial over $\mathbb{Q}$ stored as sorted exponent-coefficient
pairs), does the work there at C speed, and converts the result back to an expression.
The packed form is transient; what you see and store is always an ordinary tree.} That
uniformity is why the same \B{Part}, \B{Map}, and \B{ReplaceAll} you use on any
expression work on a polynomial too.

The section climbs from structure to solution. We begin with the anatomy of a
polynomial and the routines that read it; then expanding and collecting; then factoring,
first over the rationals and then over algebraic number fields. From there we take up
the greatest common divisor, resultants, and elimination---the machinery of eliminating
a variable---and the rational-function operations built on them. Gr\"obner bases
generalise all of this to systems. We close with equation solving: a single polynomial,
then a system, and finally \B{Reduce}, which describes a solution set completely,
including over the real numbers.

\subsection{The anatomy of a polynomial}
\label{ssec:alg-polynomials}

Before manipulating a polynomial it helps to interrogate it, and a handful of functions
read off its structure. \B{PolynomialQ} tests whether an expression \emph{is} a
polynomial in a given variable; \B{Variables} lists the indeterminates; \B{Exponent}
gives the highest power of a variable; \B{Coefficient} and \B{CoefficientList} extract
coefficients; \B{Collect} regroups terms by the powers of a chosen variable; and
\B{HornerForm} rewrites a polynomial in the nested form that evaluates with the fewest
multiplications.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/polynomials/1}{\B{PolynomialQ} confirms that $x^3-2x+1$ is a polynomial in
$x$.}
\pair{algebra/polynomials/2}{It is a genuine structural test: $\sin x + x$ is not a
polynomial, because $\sin x$ is not a power of $x$.}
\pair{algebra/polynomials/3}{\B{Variables} returns the independent variables, in
canonical order.}
\pair{algebra/polynomials/4}{\B{Exponent} is the degree in $x$---the largest power that
appears.}
\pair{algebra/polynomials/5}{\B{Coefficient}\texttt{[expr, x, 2]} pulls out the
coefficient of $x^2$; here $\binom{5}{2}=10$.}
\pair{algebra/polynomials/6}{\B{CoefficientList} returns every coefficient at once, from
the constant term upward---the fourth row of Pascal's triangle.}
\pair{algebra/polynomials/7}{\B{Collect} gathers the expression by powers of $x$,
pulling the common factor out of the linear term.}
\pair{algebra/polynomials/8}{\B{HornerForm} nests the polynomial so that evaluating it
costs three multiplications instead of six.}
\end{codepairs}

These are the lenses through which the heavier operations see a polynomial, and each of
those operations---expansion, factoring, division---returns an ordinary expression you
can hand straight back to another.

Two further views matter once there is more than one variable. \B{CoefficientList}
returns a dense array, which is wasteful for a polynomial with few terms in many
variables; \B{CoefficientRules} instead gives the \emph{sparse} representation---each
term as a rule from its exponent vector to its coefficient---and \B{MonomialList}
returns the individual monomial terms. \B{FromCoefficientRules} inverts the sparse
form.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/sparsepoly/1}{\B{MonomialList} splits the expanded cube into its four
monomial terms.}
\pair{algebra/sparsepoly/2}{\B{CoefficientRules} is the sparse view: exponent vector
\(\{i,j\}\) (the powers of \(x\) and \(y\)) mapped to its coefficient---the binomial
coefficients of \((x+y)^3\).}
\pair{algebra/sparsepoly/3}{\B{FromCoefficientRules} rebuilds the polynomial from
those rules.}
\end{codepairs}

The sparse form is the one the multivariate machinery actually uses: it is exactly
FLINT's \texttt{fmpq\_mpoly} exponent-coefficient encoding surfaced at the language
level, so a polynomial in a dozen variables with a handful of terms is stored and
manipulated in space proportional to the terms, not to the dense monomial grid.

\subsection{Expanding and collecting}
\label{ssec:alg-expand}

The most basic transformation is to \emph{expand}: to multiply out every product and
positive-integer power until the expression is a flat sum of monomials. \B{Expand} does
this at the top level; \B{ExpandAll} does it everywhere, including inside denominators;
and \B{ExpandNumerator} and \B{ExpandDenominator} expand just one part of a fraction.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/expand/1}{\B{Expand} multiplies out a power, giving the binomial
coefficients.}
\pair{algebra/expand/2}{It multiplies out products of factors just as readily.}
\pair{algebra/expand/3}{And it works in any number of variables---here the trinomial
cube, with its multinomial coefficients.}
\pair{algebra/expand/4}{\B{ExpandAll} reaches into the denominator too, distributing the
square over the numerator so the fraction becomes a sum of simpler ones.}
\pair{algebra/expand/5}{\B{ExpandNumerator} expands only the top of a fraction, leaving
the factored denominator intact.}
\pair{algebra/expand/6}{\B{ExpandDenominator} does the reverse, multiplying the
denominator into a single polynomial.}
\end{codepairs}

Expansion is the operation most likely to blow up in size---a product of $k$ factors
each with $t$ terms has up to $t^k$ terms when multiplied out---so it is also where the
packed representation earns its keep.\calloutnote{Performance}{Over the rationals
\B{Expand} routes through FLINT's \texttt{fmpq\_mpoly}~\cite{flint}, whose exponent
vectors are bit-packed and whose term merging is done with a heap; a dense expansion
that would crawl as a tree of \texttt{Plus} nodes finishes in a fraction of a second.
Because the result size is bounded in advance by the Newton polytope of the input,
Mathilda estimates it first and returns an \texttt{Overflow[]} marker---rather than
exhausting memory---when an expansion such as $(a+b+\cdots+g)^{60}$ would be too large to
hold.} The inverse move---writing a sum as a product---is factoring, and it is a great
deal deeper.

\subsection{Factoring over the rationals}
\label{ssec:alg-factor}

To \B{Factor} a polynomial is to write it as a product of \emph{irreducible} factors:
polynomials that cannot themselves be factored further. Over the rational numbers this
factorization is unique up to order and rational multiples, and Mathilda computes it
exactly. \B{FactorList} returns the same information as a list of
$\{\text{factor}, \text{multiplicity}\}$ pairs; \B{FactorTerms} pulls out only the
numeric content; and \B{FactorSquareFree} does a partial job that is worth understanding
because it is the first step of the full algorithm---\B{FactorSquareFreeList} returns
that same partial decomposition as a list of $\{\text{factor}, \text{multiplicity}\}$
pairs, the square-free analogue of \B{FactorList}.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/factor/1}{\B{Factor} splits $x^6-1$ into four irreducible pieces over
$\mathbb{Q}$: two linear and two quadratic (the cyclotomic factors).}
\pair{algebra/factor/2}{\B{FactorList} gives the factors with their multiplicities, the
leading numeric factor $2$ among them.}
\pair{algebra/factor/3}{\B{FactorSquareFree} groups the factors by multiplicity: in
$x^6-x^4-x^2+1$ it finds $x^2-1$ twice and $x^2+1$ once, but leaves $x^2-1$
\emph{unsplit}.}
\pair{algebra/factor/4}{\B{Factor} finishes the job, splitting $x^2-1$ into $x-1$ and
$x+1$: the full irreducible factorization is $(x-1)^2(x+1)^2(x^2+1)$.}
\pair{algebra/factor/5}{\B{FactorTerms} extracts only the numeric content $3$, leaving
the primitive part untouched.}
\pair{algebra/factor/6}{Factoring $x^{15}-1$ recovers the cyclotomic polynomials
$\Phi_1$, $\Phi_3$, $\Phi_5$, and $\Phi_{15}$---one for each divisor of $15$.}
\end{codepairs}

The contrast between pairs~3 and~4 is the key to how factoring actually
works.\index{square-free decomposition} A \emph{square-free} decomposition separates a
polynomial's factors by \emph{multiplicity}---which pieces occur once, which twice, and
so on---without yet splitting them into irreducibles.\calloutnote{Theory}{The tool is
the derivative. If an irreducible factor $p$ divides $f$ with multiplicity $m$, then it
divides $f'$ with multiplicity exactly $m-1$, so $\gcd(f, f')$ collects every repeated
factor with its multiplicity dropped by one. \emph{Yun's algorithm}\index{Yun's
algorithm} turns this one observation into a linear number of GCDs that peel off the
multiplicity-$1$ part, the multiplicity-$2$ part, and so on in a single sweep. It is
cheap---only GCDs and divisions---and it guarantees that the harder work to come never
has to cope with a repeated factor. Mathilda's implementation lives in
\texttt{src/poly/facpoly\_squarefree.inc}; the classical reference is
Geddes--Czapor--Labahn~\cite{gcl}.} Once the polynomial is square-free, each factor must
be split into irreducibles, and that is the genuinely hard step.\calloutnote{Under the
hood}{The classical method, \emph{Berlekamp--Zassenhaus}\index{Berlekamp--Zassenhaus
algorithm}, factors modulo a well-chosen prime $p$---a problem that is tractable over a
finite field, via the Cantor--Zassenhaus algorithm---then \emph{Hensel
lifts}\index{Hensel lifting} that factorization from $p$ to $p^2$, $p^4$, and on up to a
modulus large enough to pin down the true integer coefficients. A final recombination
step searches for subsets of the lifted factors whose product has integer coefficients
that divide the original. Mathilda carries a self-contained implementation of this
pipeline (\texttt{facpoly\_bz\_uni.inc}), and uses it, together with a Wang-style
leading-coefficient correction for the multivariate case, whenever FLINT is unavailable.
When FLINT is present---the usual case---the univariate and multivariate factorizations
over $\mathbb{Q}$ are delegated to its state-of-the-art \texttt{fmpz\_poly\_factor} and
\texttt{fmpq\_mpoly\_factor}~\cite{flint}, which turns factorizations that once hung for
tens of seconds into a few milliseconds.}

\subsection{Polynomials over algebraic number fields}
\label{ssec:alg-extensions}

Whether a polynomial is irreducible depends on the numbers you are allowed to use.
$x^2+1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$, but the moment you admit the imaginary unit it
splits into $(x-i)(x+i)$. Mathilda lets you extend the coefficient field with the
\texttt{Extension} option to \B{Factor}, adjoining one algebraic number---or a whole
tower of them---and factoring over the resulting \emph{algebraic number
field}\index{algebraic number field} $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/extensions/1}{Adjoining $i$ with \texttt{Extension -> I} splits $x^2+1$
into its two Gaussian-integer factors.}
\pair{algebra/extensions/2}{A tower: over $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)$ the quartic
$x^4-10x^2+1$---the minimal polynomial of $\sqrt2+\sqrt3$---splits into all four linear
factors.}
\pair{algebra/extensions/3}{An extension need not split a polynomial completely: over
$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$, only the $x^2-2$ part of $x^4-5x^2+6$ factors; the $x^2-3$ part
stays irreducible, since $\sqrt3\notin\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$.}
\pair{algebra/extensions/4}{\B{IrreduciblePolynomialQ} confirms $x^2+1$ is irreducible
over the rationals.}
\pair{algebra/extensions/5}{It stops being irreducible once $i$ is
adjoined.}
\pair{algebra/extensions/6}{\B{MinimalPolynomial} runs the other way, recovering the
lowest-degree rational polynomial with $\sqrt2+\sqrt3$ as a root---the very quartic of
pair~2.}
\pair{algebra/extensions/7}{\B{RootReduce} canonicalises an algebraic number; here it
recognises $\sqrt2+\sqrt3$ as the fourth root of that quartic, returned as an exact
\B{Root} object.}
\end{codepairs}

Factoring over a number field looks like it should require a whole new algorithm, but a
classical trick reduces it to factoring over the rationals, which we already
know how to do.\calloutnote{Theory}{\emph{Trager's algorithm}\index{Trager's algorithm}
rests on the \emph{norm}. For $f\in\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)[x]$, the norm
$N(f)=\operatorname{Res}_y\!\big(m_\alpha(y),\,f_y(x)\big)$---the resultant against the
minimal polynomial $m_\alpha$ of $\alpha$---is an ordinary polynomial over $\mathbb{Q}$.
Trager shows that after a suitable integer shift $x\mapsto x-s\alpha$ the norm becomes
square-free, and then each irreducible factor of $f$ over $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ is
recovered as $\gcd\big(f,\,g\big)$ in $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)[x]$ for one rational-norm
factor $g$. So the recipe is: form the norm, factor it over $\mathbb{Q}$ with the
machinery of the previous subsection, and lift each factor back by a GCD. A tower
$\mathbb{Q}(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n)$ is first collapsed to a single
\emph{primitive element}\index{primitive element theorem} $\gamma$ by the same
resultant construction, so the multi-generator case needs no new ideas. The univariate
code is in \texttt{src/poly/qafactor.c}; in any number of variables,
\texttt{src/poly/flint\_trager.c} runs the same method on FLINT, takes the shifted norm
with \texttt{fmpq\_mpoly\_resultant}, recovers the factors with a certified modular
field GCD, and checks the whole answer by exact division. A polynomial over
$\mathbb{Q}$ is first factored over $\mathbb{Q}$, and only its irreducible factors are
sent to the field.}

The number fields are one direction in which to change the coefficients; the finite
fields $\mathrm{GF}(p)$ are another, and \B{Factor} takes both, in any number of
variables:

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/factor-domains/1}{$x^4+1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$\dots}
\pair{algebra/factor-domains/2}{\dots but modulo $2$ it is $(x+1)^4$, because squaring is
additive in characteristic~$2$\index{finite field!factoring over}.}
\pair{algebra/factor-domains/3}{Modulo $3$ it splits into two quadratics. In fact $x^4+1$
is reducible modulo \emph{every} prime, though irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$---the
classic warning that a factorization mod $p$ need not lift.}
\pair{algebra/factor-domains/4}{\texttt{Extension} works for multivariate polynomials
too: $x^2-2y^2$ splits over $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$.}
\pair{algebra/factor-domains/5}{\texttt{GaussianIntegers -> True} is the extension by
$i$: $x^2+y^2=(x-iy)(x+iy)$.}
\pair{algebra/factor-domains/6}{Exponentials are generators of their own: $e^{2x}-1$ is
the difference of squares $(e^x-1)(e^x+1)$.}
\pair{algebra/factor-domains/7}{A binomial $x^{m}-1$ goes straight to its cyclotomic
factors, one per divisor of $m$: $1260$ has $36$ divisors, and the factorization of
$x^{1260}-1$ takes about a millisecond.}
\pair{algebra/factor-domains/8}{The sign convention: every polynomial factor has a
positive leading coefficient, and the sign is gathered in front.}
\end{codepairs}

\noindent The sign convention of pair~8 is the reason the outputs of
\S\ref{ssec:alg-factor} read as they do.\calloutnote{Pitfall}{``Leading'' means
lexicographically leading, over the variables in the order they first appear in the
input, so $(1-4x)(1+3y)$ comes back as $-(4x-1)(3y+1)$ printed in Mathilda's
constant-first order, \texttt{-(-1 + 4 x) (1 + 3 y)}. A single irreducible factor with no
numeric content keeps the form it came in (\texttt{Factor[1 - x]} is \texttt{1 - x}).
Over a number field the factors need not have a positive coefficient of the leading
power of~$x$: pair~4 above, and pair~3 of the previous session,
\texttt{-(Sqrt[2] - x) (Sqrt[2] + x) (-3 + x\textasciicircum 2)}, are written with the
sign elsewhere. Either way the product is an identity; only the placement of the
signs differs.}\calloutnote{Performance}{The whole \B{Factor} family was
reviewed in the autumn of 2026 against four other engines on $79$ cases in $17$
families. Plain polynomials go straight to FLINT; products and powers are factored base
by base, so $(x+1)^{500}(x-1)^{500}$ takes $0.06$~ms instead of $1.4$~s; univariate
factoring modulo a word-size prime uses FLINT's \texttt{nmod\_poly\_factor}; and a
degree-$40$ product over $\mathbb{Q}(2^{1/6})$ fell from $88$~s to $0.4$~s. Mathilda was
the fastest engine, or within $1.2\times$ of it, on every family but one, univariate
factoring modulo~$2$ at degree~$1250$, where the time is FLINT's own.} Mathilda has two complementary ways to name an
algebraic number. A \B{Root} object---``the $k$-th root of this polynomial''---is the
implicit form the equation solvers return, and \B{ToRadicals} re-expresses one in
radicals when a radical form exists. When instead you want to \emph{compute} inside a
fixed field---to add, multiply, and divide its elements and keep every result in that
same field---the explicit \B{AlgebraicNumber} representation is the right tool, and it
is the subject of the next subsection.

\subsection{Working in a number field}
\label{ssec:alg-numberfield}

The previous subsection factored \emph{over} a number field; this one computes
\emph{inside} one. \B{ToNumberField} takes an algebraic number, adjoins a generator
\(\theta\), and returns the number as an element of \(\mathbb{Q}(\theta)\) written in
the \emph{power basis}\index{power basis} \(1, \theta, \theta^2, \ldots,
\theta^{d-1}\): an \B{AlgebraicNumber} object carrying the generator and a vector of
rational coordinates. Once a number is in that form, the four field operations stay
inside the field and reduce to a canonical representative.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/numberfield/1}{\B{ToNumberField} adjoins \(\sqrt2\) and writes the
number in the power basis \(\{1,\sqrt2\}\): the coordinate vector \(\{0,1\}\) means
\(0\cdot1 + 1\cdot\sqrt2\).}
\pair{algebra/numberfield/2}{Arithmetic stays in the field and reduces---\(a^2\) is
the rational \(2\).}
\pair{algebra/numberfield/3}{\((1+\sqrt2)(1-\sqrt2) = 1-2 = -1\), again a rational.}
\pair{algebra/numberfield/4}{Division too: \(1/(1+\sqrt2) = \sqrt2-1\), the
coordinate vector \(\{-1,1\}\).}
\pair{algebra/numberfield/5}{A tower collapses to a single \emph{primitive element}:
\(\sqrt2+\sqrt3\) generates the degree-\(4\) field \(\mathbb{Q}\) adjoin a root of
\(x^4-10x^2+1\), and the number is that generator itself.}
\pair{algebra/numberfield/6}{\B{AlgebraicNumberNorm}: the field norm of \(\sqrt2\)
over \(\mathbb{Q}\), the product of its conjugates \(\pm\sqrt2\), is \(-2\).}
\pair{algebra/numberfield/7}{\B{AlgebraicNumberTrace}: the sum of the conjugates,
\(\sqrt2 + (-\sqrt2) = 0\).}
\pair{algebra/numberfield/8}{\B{NumberFieldIntegralBasis}: the ring of integers of
\(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt5)\) is spanned by \(1\) and \((1+\sqrt5)/2\)---\emph{not} by
\(\sqrt5\) alone.}
\pair{algebra/numberfield/9}{\B{AlgebraicIntegerQ} confirms the golden ratio
\((1+\sqrt5)/2\), a root of \(x^2-x-1\), is an algebraic integer.}
\pair{algebra/numberfield/10}{By contrast, \(1/2\) is not.}
\end{codepairs}

The norm and trace are not ad hoc: \(\mathbb{Q}(\theta)\) is a \(d\)-dimensional
vector space over \(\mathbb{Q}\), and multiplication by a fixed element \(\alpha\) is
a linear map on that space. Its determinant is the \B{AlgebraicNumberNorm} and its
trace the \B{AlgebraicNumberTrace}---equivalently, the product and sum of the
\(d\) conjugates of \(\alpha\).\calloutnote{Theory}{An \emph{algebraic
integer}\index{algebraic integer} is a root of a monic polynomial with \emph{integer}
coefficients; the algebraic integers of \(\mathbb{Q}(\theta)\) form a ring, its
\emph{ring of integers}\index{ring of integers}, and \B{NumberFieldIntegralBasis}
returns a \(\mathbb{Z}\)-basis for it. That ring is generally \emph{larger} than
\(\mathbb{Z}[\theta]\): for \(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt d)\) with \(d\) square-free it is
\(\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt d]\) when \(d\equiv 2,3 \pmod 4\) but
\(\mathbb{Z}\!\big[\tfrac{1+\sqrt d}{2}\big]\) when \(d\equiv 1\pmod 4\)---which is
exactly why \(\sqrt5\) yields the golden-ratio basis element above. Mathilda builds
these fields on FLINT's \texttt{qqbar} and \texttt{nf\_elem} number-field
arithmetic~\cite{flint}.} A \B{Root} object and an \B{AlgebraicNumber} name the same
numbers from opposite ends---the former implicit and canonical for solving, the latter
explicit and closed under arithmetic---and \B{RootReduce} moves between them.

\subsection{GCD, resultants, and elimination}
\label{ssec:alg-gcd}

The greatest common divisor of two polynomials is the highest-degree polynomial dividing
both---exactly the notion that lets you reduce a fraction to lowest terms. \B{PolynomialGCD}
computes it, and \B{PolynomialExtendedGCD} additionally returns the B\'ezout cofactors,
while \B{PolynomialLCM} gives the least common multiple. Division with remainder is
performed by \B{PolynomialQuotient} and \B{PolynomialRemainder} singly, or by
\B{PolynomialQuotientRemainder} for both at once. A second, subtler tool is the
\emph{resultant}\index{resultant}, computed by
\B{Resultant}, which detects a common root of two polynomials \emph{without} finding it,
and its relative the \B{Discriminant}. Together these are the elements of
\emph{elimination theory}, and \B{Eliminate} puts them to work removing a variable from
a system.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/gcd-resultant/1}{$x^3-1$ and $x^2-1$ share exactly the factor $x-1$, found
directly by \B{PolynomialGCD}---without factoring either polynomial.}
\pair{algebra/gcd-resultant/2}{\B{PolynomialExtendedGCD} also returns the B\'ezout
cofactors $\{a,b\}$ with $a f + b g = \gcd$.}
\pair{algebra/gcd-resultant/3}{\B{PolynomialQuotientRemainder} divides $x^3-1$ by $x-1$
exactly: quotient $x^2+x+1$, remainder $0$.}
\pair{algebra/gcd-resultant/4}{\B{PolynomialLCM} is the product divided by the GCD.}
\pair{algebra/gcd-resultant/5}{\B{Resultant} vanishes exactly when the two polynomials
share a root. $x^2-2$ and $x^2-3$ share none, so their resultant is the nonzero
constant $1$.}
\pair{algebra/gcd-resultant/6}{With symbolic coefficients the resultant is a polynomial
in them---the condition on $a,b,c,d$ for the two quadratics to share a root.}
\pair{algebra/gcd-resultant/7}{The \B{Discriminant} of the general quadratic is the
familiar $b^2-4ac$.}
\pair{algebra/gcd-resultant/8}{And of the depressed cubic $x^3+px+q$, the classical
$-4p^3-27q^2$.}
\pair{algebra/gcd-resultant/9}{\B{Eliminate} removes $y$ from the two equations, leaving
a single relation in $x$ alone: the projection of their common solutions onto the
$x$-axis.}
\end{codepairs}

The resultant is the determinant of the \emph{Sylvester matrix} of the two polynomials,
and equivalently the product of the differences of their roots; the discriminant of $P$
is (up to a sign and leading coefficient) the resultant of $P$ with its own derivative,
so it vanishes precisely when $P$ has a repeated root. Computing it by that determinant,
though, would be needlessly slow.\calloutnote{Under the hood}{Naive Euclidean division to
find a polynomial GCD suffers from \emph{coefficient explosion}: the intermediate
remainders acquire enormous rational coefficients even when the answer is small. The
\emph{subresultant polynomial remainder sequence}\index{subresultant PRS} tames this by
dividing out, at each step, a predicted content given by Bronstein's $\gamma/\beta/\delta$
recurrence, keeping every coefficient bounded; the resultant and the degree of the GCD
can then be read directly off the sequence. This avoids both the $(m+n)\times(m+n)$
Sylvester determinant and the blow-up of ordinary Euclid, and it is what Mathilda uses
for \B{Resultant}, \B{Subresultants}, and the GCDs beneath \B{Cancel}
(\texttt{src/poly/subresultants.c}; Geddes--Czapor--Labahn~\cite{gcl}). For plain
rational inputs the work is handed to FLINT instead.}

\subsection{Rational functions}
\label{ssec:alg-rational}

A \emph{rational function} is a ratio of polynomials, and three operations put one into a
normal form. \B{Together} combines a sum of fractions over a common denominator;
\B{Cancel} reduces a single fraction to lowest terms by dividing out the GCD of numerator
and denominator; and \B{Apart} performs the partial-fraction decomposition---the reverse
of \B{Together}, and the form you want before integrating. \B{Numerator} and
\B{Denominator} read off the two halves.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/rational/1}{\B{Together} puts a sum of fractions over the common
denominator $x(x+1)$.}
\pair{algebra/rational/2}{\B{Numerator} reads off the top of a fraction. Note that it is
\emph{structural}: the shared factor $x+1$ is not cancelled first, so the numerator is
$1+x$.}
\pair{algebra/rational/3}{\B{Denominator} likewise returns $x^2-1$, still
carrying that common factor.}
\pair{algebra/rational/4}{\B{Cancel} is what divides the shared $x+1$ out, reducing the
same fraction to $1/(x-1)$.}
\pair{algebra/rational/5}{It works over several variables, cancelling the $x-y$ from
$(x-y)/(x^2-y^2)$ to leave $1/(x+y)$.}
\pair{algebra/rational/6}{\B{Apart} splits a fraction into a sum of pieces with the
simplest possible denominators---the partial-fraction decomposition.}
\end{codepairs}

Two behaviours are worth noting. \B{Together} deliberately does \emph{not}
expand\index{rational function!factored form}: it puts the sum over one denominator and
cancels, but leaves the denominator as a product of its factors---$x(1+x)$ in pair~1,
not $x+x^2$---because expanding is a separate decision you make with \B{Expand}.
\B{Cancel} and \B{Apart} answer in the same factored form. And \B{Cancel} works by
polynomial GCD rather than by factoring, so it reduces even fractions whose numerator
and denominator are hard to factor.\calloutnote{Under the hood}{All three heads run on
one rational-function engine (\texttt{src/poly/ratfun.c}). A rational function over
$\mathbb{Q}$---in symbols, or in kernels such as \texttt{Log[x]} and
\texttt{E\textasciicircum(k x)}---is converted into FLINT once, as
$c\prod p_i^{e_i}$ with each $p_i$ primitive, and reduced there. \B{Together} combines
a sum through a \emph{gcd-free basis}\index{gcd-free basis} of the denominator factors:
pairwise coprime, square-free polynomials from which every denominator is a product of
powers. The numerator then comes from a product tree with no exact divisions, and a
single GCD against the square-free denominator settles the cancellation. \B{Cancel}
splits numerator factors against denominator factors by GCD and keeps them factored.
\B{Apart} factors the denominator, splits it by the Chinese remainder theorem and reads
each partial fraction off a $p$-adic expansion, over $\mathbb{Q}$ or over the field of
rational functions in the parameters, which replaced a symbolic linear solve:
$1/((x+a)(x+b)(x+c)(x+d)(x+f))$ went from $2$~s to under $2$~ms. One variable over
$\mathbb{Q}(i)$ or a number field is reduced natively, and \texttt{Modulus -> p} works
over $\mathrm{GF}(p)$. The factored form is for \emph{you}: when \B{Together} is called
from inside another builtin, such as \texttt{Simplify} or \B{Integrate}, it hands back
the expanded form that caller was written against.} These
partial fractions are exactly what \S\ref{sec:calculus} reaches for when integrating a
rational function.

\subsection{Gr\"obner bases}
\label{ssec:alg-groebner}

Everything so far concerned one or two polynomials. A \emph{system} of polynomials
generates an \emph{ideal}\index{ideal}---the set of all combinations
$\sum h_i f_i$---and the right way to understand that ideal is a
\B{GroebnerBasis}\index{Gröbner basis}: a canonical set of generators that answers, by a
single division, whether a given polynomial lies in the ideal, and from which the
geometry of the common solutions can be read off. The catch, and the craft, is that the
basis depends on a \emph{monomial order}\index{monomial order}---a rule for deciding
which term of a polynomial is ``leading.''

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/groebner/1}{The circle $x^2+y^2=1$ met with the line $x=y$. In the default
lexicographic order the basis is \emph{triangular}: one generator, $2y^2-1$, involves
$y$ alone, and $x-y$ then recovers $x$.}
\pair{algebra/groebner/2}{For $\{x^2-y,\;y^2-x\}$ the lexicographic basis eliminates $x$
into the single-variable $y^4-y$, whose four roots are the $y$-coordinates of the
solutions.}
\pair{algebra/groebner/3}{The same system in \texttt{DegreeReverseLexicographic} order
gives a different, more balanced basis---cheaper to compute, but no longer triangular.}
\pair{algebra/groebner/4}{Over the finite field $\mathrm{GF}(7)$, with
\texttt{Modulus -> 7}, the whole computation runs in modular arithmetic.}
\pair{algebra/groebner/5}{Elimination in action. For the parametric curve
$x=t^2,\;y=t^3$, ordering $t$ ahead of $x,y$ pushes a $t$-free generator---$x^3-y^2$---to
the front of the basis.}
\pair{algebra/groebner/6}{Naming $t$ as the variable to \emph{eliminate} (the third
argument) returns exactly that generator: $x^3-y^2=0$ is the implicit equation the
parametric points obey---the cuspidal cubic.}
\end{codepairs}

The choice of order is the whole story. The lexicographic order (pair~1) forces the
basis into a triangular, ``solved'' shape---ideal for eliminating variables and solving,
which is why it underlies system solving in \S\ref{ssec:alg-solve-systems}---but it is
the most expensive to compute, and its coefficients can grow enormously. The degree
orders (pair~3) are far cheaper but do not triangulate.\calloutnote{Theory}{The classical
method is \emph{Buchberger's algorithm}\index{Buchberger's algorithm}: for every pair of
basis polynomials form their $S$-polynomial---the combination engineered to cancel the
two leading terms---reduce it modulo the current basis, and adjoin any nonzero
remainder, until every $S$-polynomial reduces to zero. The Gebauer--M\"oller criteria
discard, in advance, the many pairs that must reduce to zero. \emph{F4}\index{F4
algorithm} (Faug\`ere) reorganises the same computation as linear algebra: it takes a
whole batch of pairs at once, writes their reductions as the rows of one sparse
\emph{Macaulay matrix}, and row-reduces it. \emph{FGLM}\index{FGLM algorithm} converts a
basis of a zero-dimensional ideal from one order to another by linear algebra in the
finite-dimensional quotient ring, which is how a cheap degree-order basis becomes a
lexicographic one. The standard text is Cox--Little--O'Shea~\cite{clo}.}

Mathilda uses all of these, chosen by the coefficient domain and the order, and lets
you pick:

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/groebner-engines/1}{The cyclic-$5$ system, a standard benchmark in five
variables.}
\pair{algebra/groebner-engines/2}{Its lexicographic basis over $\mathbb{Q}$ has $11$
elements; it is computed in a few tens of milliseconds.}
\pair{algebra/groebner-engines/3}{\texttt{Method -> "F4"} and \texttt{Method ->
"Buchberger"} reach the identical reduced basis---as they must, since a reduced Gr\"obner
basis is unique.}
\pair{algebra/groebner-engines/4}{Over $\mathrm{GF}(32003)$ the degree-reverse-lexicographic
basis has $20$ elements, computed by F4 in a few milliseconds.}
\pair{algebra/groebner-engines/5}{Machine-precision coefficients in the input give a
basis with machine-precision coefficients; the domain is read off the input.}
\pair{algebra/groebner-engines/6}{Exact input can be computed inexactly on request:
\texttt{CoefficientDomain -> InexactNumbers[20]} gives a basis with $20$-digit
coefficients.}
\pair{algebra/groebner-engines/7}{\texttt{CoefficientDomain -> RationalFunctions} treats
the other symbols, here $r$ and $t$, as parameters: the basis is over
$\mathbb{Q}(r,t)$.}
\end{codepairs}

\calloutnote{Under the hood}{Over $\mathrm{GF}(p)$ the engine is F4
(\texttt{src/poly/gb\_f4.c}): pair selection by the normal strategy with the
Gebauer--M\"oller criteria, symbolic preprocessing over a monomial hash table, and block
elimination of the Macaulay matrix mod~$p$. Over $\mathbb{Q}$ a basis is computed by
\emph{certified multi-modular lifting}\index{Gr\"obner basis!multi-modular}: F4 runs
modulo many $62$-bit primes, in batches on worker threads; the images are combined by the
Chinese remainder theorem and rational reconstruction; and the candidate is then
\emph{proved}, by checking that every input and every $S$-pair reduces to zero over
$\mathbb{Z}$ and, through its Hilbert series, that its ideal is no larger than the
input's. A wrong prime is detected and replaced, so the answer is exact, not
probable. A lexicographic basis of a zero-dimensional ideal goes degree-reverse-lex
first, then FGLM. Small systems first try a direct FLINT Buchberger, with the sugar
strategy, under a CPU-time budget. Inexact coefficients are handled by FGLM with the
linear algebra in MPFR, checked by agreement at two precisions, and the parametric
case reduces on FLINT's rational functions with a block order (lex on the variables,
degree-reverse-lex on the parameters). \texttt{Method -> "F4"} forces F4 over
$\mathbb{Q}$, \texttt{"Buchberger"} switches it off, and \texttt{"GroebnerWalk"}
walks a degree-order basis across the Gr\"obner fan to the target order. Cyclic-$7$
in degree-reverse-lex order takes about $10$~s over $\mathbb{Q}$ and $0.3$~s modulo
$2^{31}-1$.}

That triangular shape pays off directly in \emph{variable
elimination}\index{elimination!via Gr\"obner basis}. Order the variables you want gone
ahead of the ones you want to keep, and the basis elements that come out free of them
generate the \emph{elimination ideal}\index{elimination ideal}: every polynomial
consequence of the system that mentions the kept variables alone. Pairs~5 and~6 put this
to work on \emph{implicitization}\index{implicitization}. A curve traced parametrically
as $x=t^2,\;y=t^3$ is a set of points in the plane; eliminating the parameter $t$
recovers the single polynomial equation those points satisfy, $x^3=y^2$---the cuspidal
cubic. \B{GroebnerBasis} does it two ways: with $t$ ordered first, the whole basis
carries a $t$-free member (pair~5); naming $t$ in the third argument switches to an
\emph{elimination order}\index{monomial order!elimination}
(\texttt{MonomialOrder -> EliminationOrder}) and returns just the elimination ideal
(pair~6). That block order is purpose-built for the job---it need only push the
eliminated variables ahead of the kept ones, not fully order the kept block among
itself, so it reaches the elimination ideal more cheaply than a full lexicographic basis.
This is the same projection \B{Eliminate}
performs (\S\ref{ssec:alg-gcd}), now as a by-product of the basis
itself.\calloutnote{Theory}{The \emph{Elimination Theorem}\index{Elimination Theorem} is
what guarantees this. If $G$ is a Gr\"obner basis of an ideal $I$ under a
\emph{lexicographic} order with $x_1>\cdots>x_n$, then, for each $k$,
$G\cap\mathbb{Q}[x_{k+1},\ldots,x_n]$ is a Gr\"obner basis of the elimination ideal
$I\cap\mathbb{Q}[x_{k+1},\ldots,x_n]$---the consequences that mention none of the first
$k$ variables. Geometrically that intersection cuts out the (Zariski closure of the)
\emph{projection} of the solution variety onto the remaining coordinates, so eliminating
the parameters of a parametrisation yields the implicit equations of its image. This one
theorem underwrites both \B{Eliminate} and the triangular back-substitution the system
solver of \S\ref{ssec:alg-solve-systems} walks down; Cox--Little--O'Shea~\cite{clo} give
it with the implicitization algorithm.}

\subsection{Division and ideal membership}
\label{ssec:alg-polyreduce}

A Gr\"obner basis answers the ideal-membership question ``is $f$ a consequence of
these polynomials?'' by a single multivariate division, and \B{PolynomialReduce}
performs that division explicitly. Given a polynomial and a list of divisors, it
returns the \emph{cofactors}---one quotient per divisor---and the \emph{remainder}
left over.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/polyreduce/1}{Dividing $x^2+y^2$ by $\{x-y,\,x+y\}$ gives cofactors
$\{x+y,\,0\}$ and remainder $2y^2$: indeed $(x+y)(x-y) + 0\cdot(x+y) + 2y^2 =
x^2+y^2$.}
\pair{algebra/polyreduce/2}{The remainder is what decides membership. Here it is the
nonzero constant $5$, so $xy-1$ does \emph{not} lie in the ideal
$\langle x-2,\,y-3\rangle$---equivalently, $xy-1$ does not vanish at the point
$(2,3)$.}
\end{codepairs}

When the divisors form a Gr\"obner basis, this division is \emph{canonical}: the
remainder is zero exactly when the polynomial lies in the ideal, and is otherwise a
unique normal form independent of the order in which the divisors are tried. That is
the computational payoff of \S\ref{ssec:alg-groebner}---the basis turns an
undecidable-looking membership question into one division.

\subsection{Solving a polynomial equation}
\label{ssec:alg-solve-poly}

To \B{Solve} a polynomial equation is to describe every value of the unknown that
satisfies it. Mathilda factors the polynomial and dispatches each factor by degree: the
linear, quadratic, cubic, and quartic cases have closed-form solutions in radicals, and
everything else is returned as \B{Root} objects---exact algebraic numbers, indexed
canonically.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/solve-poly/1}{The general quadratic returns the quadratic formula, its two
roots exact in $a$, $b$, $c$.}
\pair{algebra/solve-poly/2}{The irreducible cubic $x^3-x-1$ has no rational roots, so by
default its three solutions are returned as \B{Root} objects.}
\pair{algebra/solve-poly/3}{The quintic $x^5-x-1$ returns five \B{Root} objects---and
here that is not a matter of taste but of mathematics: no radical formula exists.}
\pair{algebra/solve-poly/4}{A \B{Root} object is a first-class value: ``the first root of
$\#^5-\#-1$.''}
\pair{algebra/solve-poly/5}{\B{N} evaluates it to any precision, isolating the one real
root near $1.1673$.}
\pair{algebra/solve-poly/6}{\B{ToRadicals} re-expresses a root in radicals where one
exists---here the real cube root of $2$.}
\end{codepairs}

Why are the cubic and quartic returned as \B{Root} objects by default when a radical
formula \emph{does} exist for them? Because the formula is almost always monstrous. Ask
for it explicitly with the \texttt{Cubics} option and you see why:

\mtranscript{algebra/solve-cardano}

That is the same cubic $x^3-x-1$, now via \emph{Cardano's
formula}\index{Cardano's formula}, and the \B{Root} objects of pair~2 are simply a
readable name for those three numbers.\calloutnote{Theory}{Radical formulas exist for the
general polynomial up to degree four---Cardano's for the cubic (1545) and Ferrari's for
the quartic---but no further. \emph{The Abel--Ruffini theorem}\index{Abel--Ruffini
theorem} states that the general polynomial of degree five or more has no solution in
radicals: there is simply no formula in $+,-,\times,\div$ and $\sqrt[n]{\ }$ for its
roots. This is why a \B{Root} object is not a placeholder for an answer Mathilda has not
bothered to find, but frequently \emph{the} answer---the only exact, finite name the
number has. Each \B{Root}$[f, k]$ denotes a specific root of $f$ under a canonical
indexing (real roots in increasing order, then complex ones), so it is a well-defined
value that \B{N} can evaluate and arithmetic can combine.} The quartic behaves the same
way: \texttt{Quartics -> True} produces Ferrari's radical solution, which is larger
still and best left named.

Sometimes you need only to know \emph{how many} roots there are, and where.
\mcode{CountRoots}\index{CountRoots@\mcode{CountRoots}} answers that exactly, without
computing a single root:

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/countroots/1}{The quintic $x^5-3x+1$ has three real roots.}
\pair{algebra/countroots/2}{Two of them lie in $[0,2]$.}
\pair{algebra/countroots/3}{A complex range is a closed rectangle, given by its
corners; all five roots lie in $[-2,2]\times[-2i,2i]$.}
\pair{algebra/countroots/4}{Roots are counted with multiplicity: the triple root $1$
counts three times, and the roots $\pm i$ are not real.}
\end{codepairs}

\noindent The real counts come from \emph{Sturm sequences}\index{Sturm sequence}: after a
square-free factorization, the number of sign changes in the sequence
$p, p', -\operatorname{rem}(p,p'), \ldots$ drops by exactly one at each real root, so
evaluating it at the two ends of an interval counts the roots inside. On a rectangle,
roots on the boundary are counted exactly and interior ones from certified Arb root
balls~\cite{arb}. Wilkinson's notoriously ill-conditioned polynomial, perturbed so that
only ten of its roots stay real, is counted in under a millisecond.

\subsection{Solving a system}
\label{ssec:alg-solve-systems}

For a \emph{system} of polynomial equations, \B{Solve} calls on the Gr\"obner machinery
of \S\ref{ssec:alg-groebner}. It computes a lexicographic basis, which for a system with
finitely many solutions is triangular, then solves the resulting univariate polynomial
and back-substitutes---variable by variable---to recover every solution.

\mtranscript{algebra/solve-systems}

The first system has the two obvious solutions. The second is the beautiful one: the
three symmetric functions of $x,y,z$ are fixed at $6$, $11$, and $6$, which are the
coefficients of $t^3-6t^2+11t-6=(t-1)(t-2)(t-3)$, so the solutions are exactly the six
orderings of $\{1,2,3\}$. The third line exposes the mechanism: the lexicographic
Gr\"obner basis of that system has a generator $z^3-6z^2+11z-6$ in $z$ alone, then one
coupling $y$ to $z$, then one giving $x$ in terms of the rest---the triangular form that
\B{Solve} walks down.\calloutnote{Under the hood}{The pipeline (\texttt{src/solve/solvenlsys.c})
clears each equation to a numerator, computes the lexicographic basis via the Gr\"obner
walk, and---when the ideal is zero-dimensional---peels off the univariate generator in
the last variable, solves it with the single-polynomial solver of the previous
subsection, substitutes each root back, and recurses. It is careful to be
\emph{sound}: a positive-dimensional system (a curve or surface of solutions rather than
finitely many points) is reported as such rather than answered with a misleadingly
finite list, and a branch it cannot triangulate declines rather than silently dropping
solutions.} Parametric systems, with symbols that are not among the solving variables,
are handled by computing the basis over the field of rational functions in those
parameters.

\subsection{Complete solutions and the reals}
\label{ssec:alg-reduce}

\B{Solve} returns the \emph{generic} solutions, as a list of rules. But the complete
truth about an equation is often a case analysis, and that is what \B{Reduce} delivers:
not a list of solutions but a logical combination of equations and inequalities
describing the entire solution set, including the degenerate branches \B{Solve} passes
over.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/reduce/1}{The equation $ax=b$ has the solution $x=b/a$---but only when
$a\ne0$. \B{Reduce} states the complete truth, including the degenerate branch where
$a=0$ and the equation holds for all $x$ iff $b=0$.}
\pair{algebra/reduce/2}{For a genuine polynomial it gives the roots as a disjunction, the
two branches of the quadratic formula.}
\pair{algebra/reduce/3}{Over the reals it solves \emph{inequalities}: $x^2>2$ holds
exactly outside $[-\sqrt2,\sqrt2]$.}
\pair{algebra/reduce/4}{And it describes a two-dimensional region---the right half of the
unit circle---as a cell: $x$ ranges over $(0,1]$ and, for each $x$, $y$ takes the two
values $\pm\sqrt{1-x^2}$.}
\pair{algebra/reduce/5}{\B{SolveAlways} answers the dual question---for which parameter
values does $ax+b=0$ hold for \emph{every} $x$?---namely $a=b=0$.}
\end{codepairs}

The distinction is worth stating plainly: \B{Solve} is for the generic answer you want
most of the time, while \B{Reduce} is for the complete, guaranteed-exhaustive
description, degenerate cases and all. Over the reals \B{Reduce} does something genuinely
deep.\calloutnote{Theory}{Tarski proved that the theory of \emph{real-closed fields}
admits \emph{quantifier elimination}\index{quantifier elimination}: any statement built
from polynomial equations and inequalities with $\forall$ and $\exists$ is equivalent to
a quantifier-free one. Collins made it effective with \emph{cylindrical algebraic
decomposition}\index{cylindrical algebraic decomposition} (CAD): project the polynomials
down to one variable---using discriminants, resultants, and leading coefficients to track
where roots appear, merge, or vanish---build a sign diagram there, then lift it back up
one variable at a time, splitting each fibre into cells on which every polynomial has
constant sign. Mathilda uses McCallum's projection (\texttt{src/solve/reduce\_cad.c}),
and it holds itself to a hard soundness rule: if any step is undecidable---an algebraic
sign it cannot resolve, a fibre it cannot isolate---it declines rather than emit a
formula that might be wrong.} The third positional argument chooses the \texttt{Domain}:
\texttt{Reals}, \texttt{Complexes} (the default), or \texttt{Rationals}. The variable
list may also be left out, in which case \mcode{Reduce} takes every free symbol of the input
(named constants such as \texttt{Pi} excepted), in alphabetical order, and a domain
symbol may then stand in the second slot.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/reduce-infer/1}{One argument: the variable \(x\) is inferred and the
domain defaults to the complexes.}
\pair{algebra/reduce-infer/2}{A domain in the second slot is read as a domain, not as a
variable named \texttt{Reals}.}
\pair{algebra/reduce-infer/3}{A three-variable question decided outright: no point of the
triangle \(x+y+z=1\), \(x,y,z\ge0\) lies outside the sphere of radius~\(2\). The empty
region is the whole answer.}
\end{codepairs}

That decomposition can be asked for on its own. \B{CylindricalDecomposition}$[\mathit{expr},
\mathit{vars}]$ returns the CAD cells directly---always over the reals, since the cylindrical
ordering is exactly what the decomposition delivers---as the same quantifier-free formula, each
variable bounded in terms of the ones before it. It is the engine underneath \B{Reduce}'s real
solutions, surfaced by name.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/reduce/6}{The closed unit disk $x^2+y^2\le1$ laid out as a single cylindrical
cell: $x$ sweeps the interval $[-1,1]$, and for each such $x$ the fibre in $y$ is the segment
between the two arcs $\pm\sqrt{1-x^2}$.}
\end{codepairs}

\B{Reduce} is not confined to polynomials. It also solves \emph{transcendental}
equations---trigonometric, exponential, logarithmic---returning, in keeping with its
contract, the complete solution set rather than one branch.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/transcendental/1}{Over a bounded interval a trigonometric equation has
finitely many solutions, and \B{Reduce} lists them: \(\sin x = \tfrac12\) on
\([0,2\pi)\) holds at \(\pi/6\) and \(5\pi/6\).}
\pair{algebra/transcendental/2}{Unbounded, the answer is the full \emph{periodic
family}, parametrised by an integer \(C[1]\)---every odd multiple of \(\pi/2\).}
\pair{algebra/transcendental/3}{An exponential equation is solved over the complex
numbers, so every branch appears: \(e^x = 5\) at \(\log 5\) plus integer multiples of
\(2\pi i\).}
\pair{algebra/transcendental/4}{And a logarithm is inverted directly: \(\log x = 2\)
gives \(x = e^2\).}
\end{codepairs}

The periodic and multi-branch answers are the transcendental face of the same
completeness promise: where a polynomial equation has finitely many roots, a
trigonometric or exponential one has an infinite lattice of them, and \B{Reduce}
names the whole lattice with an integer parameter rather than reporting only the
principal solution. That same refusal to settle for a partial answer now reaches
\B{Simplify} acting on \B{Root} objects, whose arithmetic it collapses exactly:

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/transcendental/5}{The square of the second root of \(x^2-2\) is exactly
the rational \(2\)---\B{Simplify} recognises the algebraic identity rather than
returning to a decimal.}
\pair{algebra/transcendental/6}{And the two roots of \(x^2-2\), being \(\pm\sqrt2\),
sum to \(0\).}
\end{codepairs}

A transcendental \emph{inequality} is harder still. To decide where \(\sin x>x/2\), the
sign-diagram method of the CAD needs the points where the sign can change---the real
roots of \(x-2\sin x\)---and those have no closed form at all. Rather than give up,
\mcode{Reduce} \emph{isolates} them rigorously and names each with a transcendental
\mcode{Root} object, \texttt{Root[\{f, x0\}]}\index{Root object!transcendental}: the unique
zero of the function \(f\) in a small, certified neighbourhood of the decimal
\(x_0\).

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/reduce-transcendental-roots/1}{\(\sin x>x/2\) holds left of the negative
nonzero root of \(x=2\sin x\) and between \(0\) and the positive one. Beyond
\(|x|=2\) the sign of \(\sin x-x/2\) can no longer change, which is why finitely
many breakpoints suffice.}
\pair{algebra/reduce-transcendental-roots/2}{A \texttt{Root} you type is checked and
canonicalised: \(1.9\) is close enough to identify the root, and the object is
rewritten with its standard six-digit anchor.}
\pair{algebra/reduce-transcendental-roots/3}{It is an exact number in the same sense as
\(\sqrt2\): \mcode{N} computes it to any precision you ask.}
\pair{algebra/reduce-transcendental-roots/4}{An equation with a single real solution, the
fixed point of the cosine.}
\pair{algebra/reduce-transcendental-roots/5}{An exponential against a line: \(e^x>3x\)
fails only between the two crossings---and these crossings have a closed form.
\(e^x=3x\) is \(-x e^{-x}=-\tfrac13\), so \(x=-W(-\tfrac13)\) on the two real branches of
the Lambert \(W\) function\index{Lambert W function} (\B{ProductLog}), and \mcode{Reduce}
returns them exactly.}
\pair{algebra/reduce-transcendental-roots/6}{Numerically they are the two crossings,
\(0.619\) and \(1.512\).}
\pair{algebra/reduce-transcendental-roots/7}{Against a parabola, \(e^x=4-x^2\), no such
form exists, and the two breakpoints are certified \texttt{Root} objects.}
\end{codepairs}

The anchor \(x_0\) is a label, not an approximation the answer depends on. Behind
it Mathilda holds an interval that provably contains exactly one zero of
\(f\).\calloutnote{Under the hood}{Isolation runs on a region the engine first proves
bounded---here \(|2\sin x|\le2\), so every root of \(x-2\sin x\) has \(|x|\le2\)---using
\emph{ball arithmetic}\index{ball arithmetic} (Arb), where every quantity is a midpoint
with a rigorous error radius. A box on which \(f\) is bounded away from zero is
discarded; one on which \(f'\) is bounded away from zero holds at most one root, and a
sign change across it proves there is exactly one. The \texttt{Root[\{f, x0\}]} object
is then certified by \emph{interval Newton}\index{interval Newton method}: if one
Newton step, carried out in interval arithmetic, maps the interval strictly into
itself, it contains a unique root. \mcode{N} refines the same interval to the precision
requested. Where any of these steps fails---an interval that straddles a double root, an
unbounded region the dominance bounds cannot tame---\mcode{Reduce} declines rather than
guess, so \(\sin x>0\) over the whole line, with its infinitely many crossings, is left
unevaluated.}

Polynomial inputs have their own hard cases: a high-degree system on which the CAD's
projection grows explosively. Two features keep these honest.\calloutnote{Under the
hood}{When the region to be described is \emph{empty}, a short proof may exist that the
CAD never finds. Before running it, \mcode{Reduce} tries a \emph{sum-of-squares
certificate}\index{sum of squares!emptiness certificate}\index{Positivstellensatz}: to
show \(q<0\) is impossible on \(\{g_i\ge0\}\) it seeks a Putinar identity
\(q=s_0+\sum_i s_ig_i\) with every \(s_i\) a sum of squares. The search is numerical (a
semidefinite program over Gram matrices); the result is then rounded to rationals and
the identity and every positive-semidefiniteness condition re-verified in \emph{exact}
arithmetic, so a floating-point near-miss can never become a wrong \texttt{False}. This
is how the classical inequality
\(xy+yz+zx\ge9xyz\) on the simplex \(x+y+z=1\), \(x,y,z>0\), is proved in under a second
on this build. When no certificate is found the CAD runs as before, and its loops now
poll the clock, so \texttt{TimeConstrained[Reduce[\(\ldots\)], t]} returns
\texttt{\$Aborted} promptly and cleanly instead of overrunning its budget inside a
projection.}

\subsection{Deciding quantified statements}
\label{ssec:alg-resolve}

Tarski's theorem is more than a fact about solution sets: it makes the truth of a
\emph{fully quantified} statement decidable, and Mathilda exposes that directly. \B{ForAll}
and \B{Exists} write the quantifiers---\texttt{ForAll[x, cond, P]} and
\texttt{Exists[x, cond, P]}, the middle argument optionally restricting the bound
variable---and are inert on their own; \B{Resolve} eliminates them, returning
\texttt{True}, \texttt{False}, or, when free variables remain, the quantifier-free
condition on them.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/resolve/1}{There \emph{is} a real square root of $2$---an existential
decided \texttt{True}.}
\pair{algebra/resolve/2}{But none of $-1$: \texttt{False}, with no complex root smuggled
in. The domain is the reals.}
\pair{algebra/resolve/3}{A universal: $x^2+1$ is positive for \emph{every} real $x$.}
\pair{algebra/resolve/4}{With a free parameter the quantifier does not collapse to a
Boolean---it eliminates to a \emph{condition}. That $x^2+bx+c$ has a real root exactly
when $c\le b^2/4$ is the discriminant criterion, derived rather than recalled.}
\pair{algebra/resolve/5}{Quantifiers may alternate: for every $x$ there is a $y$ summing
to zero.}
\pair{algebra/resolve/6}{And each block may be bounded---for every $\varepsilon>0$ there
is a smaller $\delta>0$. This is the kernel of an $\varepsilon$--$\delta$ argument.}
\end{codepairs}

Pair~4 is the one to dwell on: quantifier elimination does not merely check a statement,
it \emph{computes the condition} under which a parametric one holds---and here that
condition is a theorem of elementary algebra. Pair~6 is the same alternating shape that,
carried one quantifier deeper, \emph{proves a limit} from its definition:
\S\ref{ssec:calc-limit} runs exactly this machine on the \(\varepsilon\)--\(\delta\)
statement.\calloutnote{Under the hood}{\B{Resolve} is the pure quantifier-elimination
front-end over the same CAD engine as \B{Reduce}: it eliminates the innermost quantifier
block to a quantifier-free formula, folds in that block's restriction, and repeats
outward, to any alternation depth. Because the decision procedure is complete only for
\emph{real-closed fields}, the guarantee holds for semialgebraic statements (polynomial,
rational, algebraic, and---by sign-splitting each absolute value---\B{Abs}); a
transcendental body such as \(\sin x = a\) has no polynomial decomposition, so \B{Resolve}
declines rather than guess, the same soundness contract \B{Reduce} keeps.}

\usagebox{Resolve}

Solving over the \emph{integers}---Diophantine equations---is a different world of methods
again, and it is where we turn next.

\subsection{Diophantine equations: solving over the integers}
\label{ssec:alg-diophantine}

Restrict the unknowns to whole numbers and the ground shifts beneath you. An equation
with a one-line real solution can become a deep problem in number theory, an open one,
or---provably---one that no algorithm can settle. \B{Solve} takes the restriction as its
third argument, \texttt{Integers}, and answers a \emph{Diophantine}\index{Diophantine
equation} question: which integer tuples satisfy the system. Two promises govern every
answer it gives. It is \emph{complete}---the whole solution set, never a sample: a finite
list when the solutions are finite, a parametrised family when they are infinite, and an
empty list \emph{only} as a proof that none exist. And it is \emph{sound}---when the shape
at hand is beyond its methods it returns the input unevaluated rather than guess, so an
answer it does give can be trusted.

Behind that single interface is not one algorithm but a toolbox, because there cannot be
one algorithm.\calloutnote{Theory}{\emph{Hilbert's tenth problem}\index{Hilbert's tenth
problem} asked for a procedure deciding whether a given polynomial Diophantine equation
has an integer solution. Matiyasevich, completing work of Davis, Putnam, and Robinson,
proved in 1970 that \emph{none exists}: the solvable equations coincide with the
recursively enumerable sets, so solvability is undecidable. There is thus no prospect of a
universal solver, and a system like Mathilda's is of necessity a collection of specialised
engines---each recognising a family it can settle completely, with a principled decline
everywhere else.} \B{Solve}\texttt{[\ldots, Integers]} reads the shape of the input and
dispatches to the matching engine. What follows is a tour of the classical ones.

The simplest case, a single \emph{linear} equation, is completely understood.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/diophantine/1}{$5x+3y=1$: the extended Euclidean algorithm finds one
solution, and the whole family follows---a line of integer points stepped out by the
parameter $C[1]$.}
\pair{algebra/diophantine/2}{$6x+9y=5$ has \emph{no} solution: the left side is always a
multiple of $\gcd(6,9)=3$, which does not divide $5$. The empty list says exactly that.}
\pair{algebra/diophantine/3}{A linear \emph{system} is solved the same way through the
Hermite normal form---a particular solution plus an integer basis for the kernel lattice.}
\end{codepairs}

A linear Diophantine equation $a_1x_1+\cdots+a_nx_n=c$ is solvable precisely when
$\gcd(a_1,\ldots,a_n)\mid c$, and its solutions then form a lattice: one particular point
translated by the integer combinations of $n-1$ basis vectors. Mathilda reads that lattice
off the gcd staircase, and a system off its \B{HermiteDecomposition}, so an unsolvable
equation is rejected instantly and with certainty by the divisibility test alone.

Degree two opens onto the classical theory of quadratic forms, and two shapes stand
out---sums of squares, and Pell's equation. The integer right triangles are the solutions
of $x^2+y^2=z^2$:

\mtranscript{algebra/diophantine-pythagorean}

Ask for the triples below thirty and the familiar $(3,4,5)$ and $(5,12,13)$ appear beside
their multiples. Drop the bound and \B{Solve} returns instead the complete two-parameter
family---$x=2C_1C_2C_3$, $y=C_3(C_1^2-C_2^2)$, $z=C_3(C_1^2+C_2^2)$---Euclid's
parametrisation, every triple and nothing but triples. Pell's equation $x^2-Dy^2=1$ is
subtler:

\mtranscript{algebra/diophantine-pell}

For $D=61$ the smallest positive solution is already the ten-digit
$(1766319049,\,226153980)$---the very case Fermat posed as a challenge in 1657, knowing
how brutally large the answer is. Bound $x$ and \B{Solve} reports that fundamental
solution; remove the bound and it hands back the entire infinite orbit as a
\B{ConditionalExpression} family in $C[1]$; ask \B{FindInstance} instead and you get
a single witness. All three come from one classical construction.\calloutnote{Theory}{The
solutions of Pell's equation $x^2-Dy^2=1$, for $D$ a positive non-square, are governed by
the \emph{continued fraction}\index{continued fraction!Pell's equation} of $\sqrt D$. Its
expansion is eventually periodic, and the convergent at the end of the first period gives
the \emph{fundamental solution} $(x_1,y_1)$; every other solution is then
$x_k+y_k\sqrt D=(x_1+y_1\sqrt D)^k$, so one continued fraction generates the whole infinite
family. The size of $(x_1,y_1)$ is governed by the period length, which for $D=61$ is
long---hence the ten-digit fundamental solution from so small a $D$. Mathilda computes it
with \B{ContinuedFraction} and returns the orbit in closed form; the same machinery, with
Nagell's bound on the class representatives, handles the general $x^2-Dy^2=N$ and the
negative Pell $x^2-Dy^2=-1$ (which exists only when the period is odd).} Not every
quadratic form has solutions at all, and the system will \emph{prove} it when they are
absent:

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/diophantine/4}{$x^2+y^2=3z^2$ returns only the origin. That is Mathilda's
way of certifying that no \emph{nontrivial} integer point exists---a proof, not a failure
to find one.}
\end{codepairs}

\noindent The verdict is \emph{Legendre's theorem}\index{Legendre's theorem}: a
homogeneous ternary quadratic $ax^2+by^2+cz^2=0$ has a nonzero integer solution iff the
obvious sign condition holds and $-bc$, $-ca$, $-ab$ are quadratic residues modulo $a$,
$b$, $c$ respectively---conditions Mathilda checks directly with the Legendre symbol.
When they pass, the form is a genus-zero curve and its \emph{complete} solution is a
rational parametrisation swept out by the chord construction; when they fail, the lone
origin is returned as the certificate of impossibility.

Past degree two, each classical equation demands its own deep theorem, and \B{Solve}
reaches for the right one.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/diophantine/5}{Ramanujan's taxicab: $1729$ is the least number expressible
as a sum of two positive cubes in two ways, $1^3+12^3=9^3+10^3$.}
\pair{algebra/diophantine/6}{$x^3+y^3+z^3=4$ is impossible, and proved so with no search
at all: every cube is $0$ or $\pm1$ modulo $9$, so a sum of three can never be $\equiv4$.}
\pair{algebra/diophantine/7}{The Mordell curve $y^2=x^3-2$ has only the integer points
$(3,\pm5)$---a fact first claimed by Fermat---found by descent in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-2}]$.}
\pair{algebra/diophantine/8}{The Ramanujan--Nagell equation $2^n-7=x^2$ has exactly five
solutions, the largest the striking $181^2+7=2^{15}$.}
\pair{algebra/diophantine/9}{Brocard's problem $n!+1=m^2$ is still open in general;
Mathilda finds the three known \emph{Brown numbers} $n=4,5,7$ within the search window.}
\pair{algebra/diophantine/10}{Catalan's conjecture: the only consecutive perfect powers
are $8$ and $9$, so $3^2-2^3=1$ is the unique solution of $x^a-y^b=1$.}
\pair{algebra/diophantine/11}{Fermat's Last Theorem, returned in an instant: $x^3+y^3=z^3$
has no positive solution, and \B{Solve} answers \texttt{\{\}} before searching at all.}
\pair{algebra/diophantine/12}{A Thue equation, $x^3-2y^3=1$: an irreducible binary cubic
set equal to a constant has only finitely many integer points, here just two.}
\end{codepairs}

Several of these answers are theorems standing in for a search.\calloutnote{Under the
hood}{The dispatcher recognises each shape and calls a dedicated engine. \emph{Fermat's
Last Theorem} (Wiles, 1995) and the mod-$9$ obstruction let $x^n+y^n=z^n$ and the
unsolvable sums of three cubes return \texttt{\{\}} with no search. \emph{Catalan's
equation} is settled by \emph{Mihailescu's theorem} (2002). The \emph{Mordell} curve
$y^2=x^3+k$ is solved by factoring $x^3=(y-\sqrt k)(y+\sqrt k)$ in the ring
$\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt k]$ and reading off the cubes. \emph{Ramanujan--Nagell} reduces to a
Lucas sequence bounded by the Bilu--Hanrot--Voutier primitive-divisor theorem. The
\emph{Thue} solver (Tzanakis--de Weger) is the deepest: it builds the number field
$\mathbb{Q}(\theta)$ of a root of $F(t,1)$, turns the equation into one about units, bounds
the unit exponents by \emph{Baker's linear forms in logarithms}, shrinks that astronomical
bound to something enumerable with \emph{LLL} lattice reduction, and verifies each
candidate exactly. And the celebrated sums of three cubes---$33$, $42$---use A.~R.~Booker's
cube-root-mod-$d$ method. The engines live under \texttt{src/solve/solveint*.c} and
\texttt{solvethue.c}.} What unites them is the completeness promise: a finite list is
\emph{every} solution, and an empty one is a proof.

That promise is also where honesty is required, and the two ways it can bite are worth
seeing plainly. First, the toolbox is deliberately incomplete---by Hilbert's tenth problem
it must be---so an unrecognised equation is declined, not guessed:

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/diophantine/13}{Strip Pell of its constraints and Mathilda declines. The
solution orbit is unbounded, and rather than fabricate or silently truncate a family it
cannot name, it returns the input unevaluated.}
\end{codepairs}

\noindent Second, an empty result is a proof only over the range you actually
asked about.\calloutnote{Pitfall}{When you supply an explicit bound, \texttt{\{\}} means
``no solutions in that box,'' not ``no solutions anywhere.'' Sums of three cubes are the
cautionary tale: $x^3+y^3+z^3=42$ looks hopeless under any modest search, yet it \emph{is}
solvable---the smallest solution, found only in 2019, is a sum of three sixteen-digit
cubes. A bounded search that returns \texttt{\{\}} has proved something true and useful
(nothing small works), but not the global statement. The proofs that \emph{do} hold
unconditionally are the ones resting on an obstruction or a theorem---the mod-$9$ argument
for $4$, Wiles for $x^n+y^n=z^n$---not on an exhausted box.}

\subsection{Simplifying under assumptions}
\label{ssec:alg-refine}

Many of the identities a computer algebra system knows hold only \emph{conditionally}.
$\sqrt{x^2}$ equals $x$ when $x\ge0$ but $|x|$ in general; $\log(xy)$ splits into
$\log x+\log y$ only when the factors are positive; $\sin(n\pi)$ vanishes only when $n$
is an integer. Left to itself Mathilda will not assume what you have not told it, and so
it keeps the general form. \B{Refine} is how you tell it: \texttt{Refine[expr, assum]}
gives the form \(\mathit{expr}\) would take if its symbols satisfied the assumptions
\(\mathit{assum}\)\index{assumptions!simplifying under}\index{simplification!under
assumptions}---an equation, an inequality, a domain membership through \B{Element},
or any logical combination of these.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/refine/1}{With $x>0$ the radical loses its absolute value: $\sqrt{x^2}=x$.}
\pair{algebra/refine/2}{Knowing only that $x$ is real, the honest answer is $|x|$---the
one form that holds for either sign.}
\pair{algebra/refine/3}{\B{Abs} resolves the other way under $x<0$, where $|x|=-x$.}
\pair{algebra/refine/4}{The logarithm of a product splits once a factor is known
positive: $\B{Log}$ of $xy$ becomes $\log x+\log y$.}
\pair{algebra/refine/5}{\B{Assuming} puts a fact into scope for everything inside it, so
the two sign assumptions combine---$\sqrt{x^2y^2}=|x|\,|y|=-xy$ when $x>0$ and $y<0$.}
\pair{algebra/refine/6}{Sign facts follow along a chain of inequalities: $a>b>0$ gives
$a>0$ by transitivity, so $\sqrt{a^2}=a$.\index{assumptions!inequality chains}}
\end{codepairs}

Refine does more than rewrite. It will \emph{decide} a relation---an equation, an
inequality, a membership---when the assumptions settle it, returning \texttt{True} or
\texttt{False} in place of the relation itself.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/refine-relations/1}{A square is nonnegative for every real $x$---a fact
Refine proves rather than takes on faith.}
\pair{algebra/refine-relations/2}{Equations fall to substitution and a zero test: $a=b$
follows from $a-b=0$.}
\pair{algebra/refine-relations/3}{And a genuinely nonlinear positivity holds---the
quadratic form $x^2-xy+y^2+1$ is strictly positive over the reals, so its \B{Sign} is
$1$.}
\end{codepairs}

\calloutnote{Under the hood}{Refine shares its assumption engine with \texttt{Simplify}
(the internal \texttt{apply\_assumption\_rules} pass): every rewrite Refine performs is
one Simplify would also perform. The difference is the search. \texttt{Simplify} wraps
those rewrites in a complexity-minimising search over many candidate forms and keeps the
smallest; Refine is the rewrite-and-decide pass \emph{without} that search, so it is
faster and more predictable but will not, for instance, factor. Relational decisions run
through the same \texttt{Reduce}/CAD entailment that produced the complete real solution
sets earlier in this section: a predicate $P$ is \texttt{True} exactly when
$\mathit{assum}\wedge\lnot P$ is unsatisfiable over the reals, and quantities appearing
algebraically in an inequality are taken to be real.}

\calloutnote{Pitfall}{Refine is \emph{sound}. When the assumptions do not settle a
question it returns the expression or relation unchanged rather than guess a branch, and
under a coupling assumption such as $a-b=0$ its zero test never reports a true identity as
nonzero---it downgrades to undecided sooner than sample the very points the assumption
forbids. An answer Refine does give can therefore be trusted.}

\usagebox{Refine}

The same assumptions reach \mcode{Simplify}, given as its second argument, and there they
do more than resolve a sign: they unlock whole \emph{conditional identities}, equalities
that are false in general but true on the region you describe. The variety is the point.
An absolute value whose argument has a fixed sign, an inverse-trigonometric
multiple-angle formula valid only on one branch, a power of \(-1\) whose exponent's
parity is all that matters, the arithmetic of \B{Floor} and \B{Ceiling} on integers---each
is a theorem about a set, not about every complex number.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/simplify-assumptions/1}{On \(0<x<1\) the quantity \(x-2\) is negative
throughout, so \(|x-2|=2-x\).}
\pair{algebra/simplify-assumptions/2}{Widen the region to \(0<x<3\) and \(x-2\) changes
sign inside it. No single branch is correct, and the absolute value stays.}
\pair{algebra/simplify-assumptions/3}{\(\arccos(1-2x^2)=2\arcsin x\) is the double-angle
formula read backwards; it holds for \(0\le x\le1\) and fails for negative \(x\), where
the left side is still positive.}
\pair{algebra/simplify-assumptions/4}{For an integer \(n\), \(n^2\) and \(n\) have the
same parity, which is all a power of \(-1\) sees.}
\pair{algebra/simplify-assumptions/5}{\(\lfloor n/2\rfloor+\lceil n/2\rceil=n\): split by
the parity of \(n\) and both halves agree.}
\pair{algebra/simplify-assumptions/6}{Consecutive integers are coprime, so
\(\gcd(n,n+1)=1\).}
\pair{algebra/simplify-assumptions/7}{\(P_n(-1)=(-1)^n\) for the Legendre polynomials
(\B{LegendreP}), a fact about integer degree only.}
\pair{algebra/simplify-assumptions/8}{Periodicity over a symbolic multiple of \(\pi\): the
shift \(2n\pi+\pi\) is an \emph{odd} multiple whenever \(n\) is an integer, so the sine
changes sign.}
\pair{algebra/simplify-assumptions/9}{\mcode{Refine} pulls a squared factor out of a radical
once its sign is known, even when the square is hidden in a quotient:
\(\sqrt{1+b^2/a^2}=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}/a\) for \(a>0\).}
\pair{algebra/simplify-assumptions/10}{No assumption is needed when the argument is an
exact constant. \(\pi-4\) is \emph{certified} negative by ball arithmetic, so the absolute
value resolves to \(4-\pi\).}
\end{codepairs}

Pair~2 is as instructive as pair~1. Simplify did not reach for the more common branch or
for the larger half of the interval; it left \(|x-2|\) alone because neither \(x-2\) nor
\(2-x\) is right everywhere on \((0,3)\). Every rewrite on this page is either proved on
the whole region or not made.\calloutnote{Theory}{How is a conditional identity
\emph{proved}?\index{assumptions!proving a conditional identity} For a sign, Mathilda
runs a \emph{sign oracle}\index{sign oracle}: cheap structural provers first (a sum of
positive terms is positive), then a decomposition of the trigonometric family
(\(\operatorname{sign}\tan=\operatorname{sign}\sin\cdot\operatorname{sign}\cos\)), and
finally the real decision procedure of \S\ref{ssec:alg-reduce}: \(g\ge0\) holds on the
region \(A\) exactly when \(A\wedge g<0\) has no real solution, and \mcode{Reduce} can
\emph{prove} that emptiness. A candidate identity \(f=h\), such as \(\arccos(1-2x^2)=
2\arcsin x\), goes the same way: Simplify proposes a short list of candidate right-hand
sides and keeps the first for which \(A\wedge f\ne h\) reduces to \texttt{False}.
Integer assumptions use a different tool, arithmetic modulo~\(2\): an integer-valued
exponent polynomial is reduced over the Boolean ring (\(g^2=g\)), which is why
\((-1)^{n^2}\) and \((-1)^{n^3+n}\) collapse without any case split. In every case a
\texttt{False} from the oracle is a proof, and anything less is a decline.} For an
expensive input, Simplify's \texttt{TimeConstraint} option bounds the whole call, every
internal phase included; when the budget runs out you get the best form found so far
rather than a hang.

A related family needs no assumptions at all, because the variables are gone: the
values of the trigonometric functions at rational multiples of \(\pi\). Each such value
is an \emph{algebraic number}---\(\cos(k\pi/n)\) is half the sum of two \(2n\)-th roots
of unity---so products and sums of them can be decided exactly in a number field.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/trig-algebraic/1}{Left alone, the product is three separate cosines.}
\pair{algebra/trig-algebraic/2}{\mcode{Simplify} recognises it as the rational \(1/8\), a
classical identity proved here by exact computation with roots of unity.}
\pair{algebra/trig-algebraic/3}{The tangent analogue is irrational: \(\sqrt7\).}
\pair{algebra/trig-algebraic/4}{\mcode{RootReduce} hands back \(\sin(\pi/7)\) as an explicit
algebraic number, the fourth (in increasing order) of the six real roots of its minimal polynomial.}
\pair{algebra/trig-algebraic/5}{The logarithmic form of the Gudermannian inverse,
\(\log(\sec x+\tan x)\), does \emph{not} collapse unconditionally: at \(x=3\) its argument
is negative and the logarithm picks up \(i\pi\).}
\pair{algebra/trig-algebraic/6}{On \(-\pi/2<x<\pi/2\), where \(\sec x+\tan x>0\),
\mcode{FullSimplify} rewrites it as \(\operatorname{artanh}(\sin x)\).}
\end{codepairs}

\subsection{Simplification beyond polynomials}
\label{ssec:alg-simplify-engines}

\B{Simplify} is a search: it tries many transformations and keeps the form it scores
simplest. The transformations are where the mathematics lives, and for polynomials and
rational functions they are the operations of this section. Beyond polynomials, three
kinds of knowledge matter: identities among the \emph{special functions}, the arithmetic
of \emph{radicals}, and the structure of polynomials in several sets of variables. Some
of it is so cheap that the evaluator applies it without being asked.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/simplify-engines/1}{Not a \texttt{Simplify} at all: powers with one
symbolic exponent merge their numeric bases on evaluation, so $2^a3^a5^{-a}$ is
$(6/5)^a$.}
\pair{algebra/simplify-engines/2}{$\Gamma(n+2)/\Gamma(n)=n(n+1)$, by the recurrence
$\Gamma(z+1)=z\Gamma(z)$ applied twice.}
\pair{algebra/simplify-engines/3}{Euler's reflection formula
$\Gamma(x)\Gamma(1-x)=\pi/\sin\pi x$.}
\pair{algebra/simplify-engines/4}{At $x=\tfrac13$ the same formula gives an exact
constant.}
\pair{algebra/simplify-engines/5}{The Bessel recurrence
$J_{n+1}(z)+J_{n-1}(z)=\tfrac{2n}{z}J_n(z)$.}
\pair{algebra/simplify-engines/6}{The digamma function's recurrence
$\psi(x+1)-\psi(x)=1/x$.}
\pair{algebra/simplify-engines/7}{The defining identity of the Lambert $W$ function,
$W(z)e^{W(z)}=z$.}
\end{codepairs}

These are not entered one by one.\index{simplification!special functions}\calloutnote{Under
the hood}{\texttt{Simplify} treats special functions the way \B{Together} treats rational
functions. A pass in \texttt{src/simp/simp\_sf\_engine.c} groups the special-function
atoms of an expression into \emph{families}: the same head and the same fixed
arguments, with shift arguments that differ by integers, such as $J_{n-1}$, $J_n$ and
$J_{n+1}$. Each family is rewritten onto one basis through \emph{affine shift
matrices}---a descriptor states only the raising relation, and the engine inverts it to
step down---and then reflections, multiplication formulas and Wronskian identities are
applied before \B{Together}, \B{Expand} and \B{Factor} decide the result. Descriptor
modules cover the Bessel functions, \texttt{Gamma} and its relatives
(\texttt{Pochhammer}, \texttt{Beta}, \texttt{Binomial}, \texttt{BarnesG}), the zeta and
polygamma family, the error and exponential integrals, the hypergeometric functions,
the orthogonal polynomials, Airy, Struve and Whittaker functions, and the elliptic
integrals, \texttt{ProductLog} and \texttt{QPochhammer}. A rewrite is kept only when the
score strictly drops, or when it eliminates special-function atoms at a bounded score
cost, and every identity is gated to the region where it holds, found by numeric probes
in all four quadrants and on the branch cuts.}

Radicals are the second kind. A nested radical sometimes \emph{denests}, and finding out
when is a question in Galois theory\index{radical!denesting}:

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/simplify-engines/8}{Ramanujan's identity
$\sqrt[3]{\sqrt[3]2-1}=\tfrac13\big(\sqrt[3]{12}+\sqrt[3]{3}-\sqrt[3]{6}\big)$,
printed with $\sqrt[3]{12}/3=(2/3)^{2/3}$ and $\sqrt[3]3/3=1/3^{2/3}$.}
\pair{algebra/simplify-engines/9}{\B{FunctionExpand} writes $\cos(2\pi/17)$ in square
roots---Gauss's proof that the regular $17$-gon is constructible with ruler and
compass.}
\end{codepairs}

\calloutnote{Theory}{A radical $r^{1/n}$ with $r$ in the field $K$ generated by the
radicals inside it denests exactly when $r^{1/n}=c^{1/n}u$ with $c\in\mathbb{Q}$ and
$u\in K$. Kummer theory\index{Kummer theory} limits the candidates for $c$ to products
of the primes in the radicands and of $2$, $3$ and $5$, and a congruence on the norm of
$r$ prunes the list further. For each surviving $c$, Mathilda
finds $u$ as an integer relation, by LLL lattice reduction on $320$-bit values, and then
\emph{proves} it by checking $cu^n=r$ in exact arithmetic. The cosine of pair~9 is
computed from \emph{Gauss periods}\index{Gauss period}: sums of roots of unity over the
subgroups of the cyclic Galois group $(\mathbb{Z}/17)^\times$, which halve at each step,
each child period being $(\eta\pm\sqrt\Delta)/2$ for an integer $\Delta$. The same
descent handles the $257$-gon in about $0.2$~s.}

The third kind is structure in a polynomial of many variables that neither expansion
nor factoring shows. \mcode{SeparablePolynomialDecomposition}\index{SeparablePolynomialDecomposition@\mcode{SeparablePolynomialDecomposition}}%
\texttt{[p, S, T]} writes $p$ as a shortest sum of products $B(S)\,Q(T)$, a factor in
the variables $S$ times a factor in the variables $T$:

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/simplify-engines/10}{The expanded polynomial in $a,b,x,y$ is recovered as
a sum of two separated products. The number of terms is the rank of the matrix of
coefficients indexed by the monomials in $S$ and in $T$.}
\end{codepairs}

\noindent A rank-one answer would be a product of a factor in $S$ and a factor in $T$,
which is simply a factorization; the decomposition earns its keep when the rank is two or
more. \texttt{FullSimplify} tries the decomposition for you, on an exact polynomial of
at least four terms in two to five variables, over every split of the variables, and
keeps it when it is a genuine sum of separated products and the result is smaller:

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/simplify-engines/11}{A polynomial of rank two in the split
$\{a,b\}$, $\{x,y\}$, expanded.}
\pair{algebra/simplify-engines/12}{It has $251$ leaves expanded, and
\texttt{Simplify} brings it only to $197$\ldots}
\pair{algebra/simplify-engines/13}{\ldots while \texttt{FullSimplify} finds the
separated form, with each product factored\ldots}
\pair{algebra/simplify-engines/14}{\ldots in $38$ leaves.}
\end{codepairs}

\calloutnote{Performance}{The decomposition is a \texttt{FullSimplify} transform, not a
\texttt{Simplify} one, by design. It was a \texttt{Simplify} stage until v0.6178, where
it was measured adding $26\%$ to \texttt{Simplify}'s time on a $63$-case polynomial
corpus ($0.78$~s to $0.98$~s), a cost paid on every polynomial for a form that few of
them have. So
\texttt{Simplify} stays fast and leaves the search to \texttt{FullSimplify}, the
function whose contract is to try harder; or call
\mcode{SeparablePolynomialDecomposition} yourself when you know the split.}
\index{FullSimplify@\mcode{FullSimplify}!separable decomposition}

\subsection{Is it zero?}
\label{ssec:alg-possiblezeroq}

Beneath \B{Refine}, beneath \texttt{Simplify}, beneath every exact elimination in linear
algebra sits one question, the simplest to ask and the hardest to answer: \emph{is this
expression zero?} When the expression is a plain number it is trivial; when it is built
from radicals, logarithms, exponentials, and trigonometric functions it is, in full
generality, unanswerable. \B{PossibleZeroQ}\index{zero testing!PossibleZeroQ} is
Mathilda's working answer---a fast, self-contained test that reports \texttt{True} when
symbolic and numeric evidence agree an expression vanishes, and \texttt{False} otherwise.
Its strength is that it sees through identities the evaluator leaves untouched.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/possiblezeroq/1}{The Pythagorean identity does not auto-simplify, yet the
zero test recognises it.}
\pair{algebra/possiblezeroq/2}{So does a nested-radical identity: \(\sqrt{3+2\sqrt2}\) really
is \(1+\sqrt2\).}
\pair{algebra/possiblezeroq/3}{A logarithm identity, \(\log 6=\log 2+\log 3\).}
\pair{algebra/possiblezeroq/4}{And Euler's identity \(e^{i\pi}=-1\).}
\end{codepairs}

The verdicts are not symmetric, and the asymmetry is the key to using the test well.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/possiblezeroq/5}{A genuine non-zero is reported \texttt{False}.}
\pair{algebra/possiblezeroq/6}{So is a free symbol, which is not \emph{identically}
zero.}
\end{codepairs}

Under the hood the test runs cheap exact stages first---structural, rational, and a
trig/exponential normaliser. A \emph{structural non-zero
certificate}\index{zero testing!non-zero certificate} then settles many non-vanishing forms
outright, with no numeric work at all: the exponential is entire and has no zeros, so a
product of exponentials and finite non-zero constants cannot be zero.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/possiblezeroq-limits/1}{\(-e^{-ax}\) is never zero, and the certificate proves
it \texttt{False} at once---undeceived by the decay toward \(0\) as \(ax\) grows, which a
purely numeric probe (sampling at ever larger arguments) could mistake for an identity.}
\end{codepairs}

When the exact stages do not settle it, the test falls back to numeric sampling: it
evaluates the expression at several points, at rising precision, in the spirit of the
Schwartz--Zippel\index{Schwartz--Zippel} lemma. It deliberately never calls
\texttt{Simplify} (which would be far more expensive, and itself relies on the zero test).
This division fixes the meaning of the two answers. A \texttt{False} is backed by the
certificate or by an explicit point at which the expression is plainly non-zero, so it is
\emph{reliable}: the expression is not identically zero. A \texttt{True} is the heuristic
verdict---strong evidence, not a proof. The same numeric machinery lets \mcode{Equal} and
\mcode{Unequal} decide relations between numeric constants, and it is the strong setting for
the \texttt{ZeroTest} option of \B{RowReduce} and \B{NullSpace} (\S\ref{ssec:la-zerotest}).

Assumptions narrow the sampler to the region they describe, so an identity that holds only
there is recognised.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/possiblezeroq/7}{With nothing known, \(\sqrt{x^2}-x\) is not identically
zero---it fails for negative \(x\).}
\pair{algebra/possiblezeroq/8}{Told \(x>0\), the sampler draws only positive points and the
identity stands.}
\end{codepairs}

\calloutnote{Performance}{The reason \mcode{PossibleZeroQ} is the first-line test, and not
\texttt{Simplify}, is cost. Its work is \emph{bounded}---a fixed budget of samples at a few
precisions---no matter how the expression is built, whereas \texttt{Simplify}'s symbolic
search is open-ended and can explode. On the Machin-like identity below,
\mcode{PossibleZeroQ} returns \texttt{True} in about half a millisecond, while
\texttt{Simplify} spends some fifteen times longer proving it; on the nested radical above
the zero test is roughly five times faster. The trade is honest, though: on an easy identity
such as the triple-angle formula the two are comparable, and \texttt{Simplify}, when it
succeeds, hands back the \emph{proof}---the literal \(0\)---that \mcode{PossibleZeroQ} only
makes probable. (Figures from this build, Apple M-series.)}

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{algebra/possiblezeroq-limits/2}{\(\arctan\tfrac13+\arctan\tfrac12=\tfrac\pi4\): the
zero test decides it at once.}
\pair{algebra/possiblezeroq-limits/3}{\texttt{Simplify} reaches the literal \(0\) too,
by an exact argument. Each \(\arctan(p/q)\) is the angle of the Gaussian integer \(q+pi\),
so \(e^{i e}\) is an algebraic number: here \((3+i)(2+i)=5+5i\), whose angle is
\(\tfrac\pi4\). It checks \(e^{ie}=1\) exactly, bounds \(e\) numerically to pin the
multiple of \(2\pi\), and concludes \(e=0\)---a proof, where the zero test's \texttt{True}
is evidence.}
\end{codepairs}

\calloutnote{Pitfall}{A \texttt{True} is evidence, not a proof. The structural certificate
disposes of whole families of non-vanishing expressions exactly, but where the answer rests
on numeric sampling the verdict is a strong probability, not a certainty---by Richardson's
theorem (below) no test can be complete, and a sufficiently pathological non-zero can still
slip past a finite set of samples. So never let a \texttt{True} \emph{certify} a result you
need to be exact---to accept a candidate antiderivative, say; when you need certainty, prove
it by having \texttt{Simplify} reduce the difference to the literal \(0\). A \texttt{False}
is the reliable direction.}

\calloutnote{Theory}{That no zero test can be perfect is a theorem, not a shortfall of
effort. \emph{Richardson's theorem}\index{Richardson's theorem} shows that for expressions
built from the rationals, \(\pi\), \(\log\), \(\exp\), the sine, and absolute value,
deciding whether an expression is identically zero is \emph{undecidable}. Every computer
algebra system therefore settles for an incomplete test---one that may fail to recognise a
genuine zero, and, when it leans on numeric evidence as \mcode{PossibleZeroQ} does, may
occasionally over-report one. Making the choice of test explicit is the honest design: a
structural default that is fast and never wrong but often blind, and \mcode{PossibleZeroQ}
or \texttt{Simplify} at the stronger, dearer end.}

\usagebox{PossibleZeroQ}

That is the arc of algebra in Mathilda: from reading a polynomial's structure, through
expanding and factoring it---over the rationals and over the number fields you choose to
work in---through the GCDs, resultants, and Gr\"obner bases that eliminate variables, to
solving single equations, systems, the complete real solution sets that \B{Reduce}
describes, the integer solutions that the Diophantine engines find or prove absent, the refinement of
an expression under whatever you know about its symbols, and the zero test that quietly
underlies every one of these decisions. The
rational-function normal forms established here feed directly into the integration of
\S\ref{sec:calculus}. Coaxing an expression into its \emph{simplest} form is the more
general art that builds on all of this: \texttt{Refine}, just seen, applies a set of
assumptions directly, while \texttt{Simplify}'s complexity-minimising search over many
candidate forms is taken up later. As always, the exhaustive options for any function
named here are one \texttt{?Name} away at the prompt.
