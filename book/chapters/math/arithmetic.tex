\section{Arithmetic}
\label{sec:arithmetic}

Arithmetic is the floor everything else stands on, and Mathilda takes it seriously.
Rather than one kind of number, it keeps a small \emph{tower} of numeric\index{number!tower of representations}
representations and moves between them automatically:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Machine integers}\index{number!machine integer} — signed 64-bit words, the hardware's native
        integer, used while a value is small enough to fit.
  \item \textbf{Arbitrary-precision integers}\index{number!arbitrary-precision integer} — GMP~\cite{gmp} ``bignums''\index{bignum}, into
        which a machine integer is promoted the instant it would overflow, and out
        of which a result is demoted the instant it fits again.
  \item \textbf{Exact rationals}\index{number!rational} — pairs of exact integers, kept in lowest terms.
  \item \textbf{Machine reals}\index{number!machine real} — IEEE-754 double-precision floating point\index{IEEE 754}, the fast
        approximate numbers of the hardware.
  \item \textbf{Arbitrary-precision reals}\index{number!arbitrary-precision real} — MPFR~\cite{mpfr} values\index{MPFR}, correctly
        rounded to as many digits as you ask for.
\end{itemize}

The whole of this section is about how these five representations behave and how
they hand off to one another. We begin with the way numbers are typed and the
operators that combine them; then we take the representations in two groups---the
machine numbers (fast, fixed-size) and the arbitrary-precision numbers (exact integers
and rationals, and MPFR reals)---before turning to complex numbers and the everyday
functions that round, divide, and dissect a number. We close with interval arithmetic,
which computes with \emph{guaranteed enclosures}\index{interval arithmetic!enclosure}
rather than points.

\subsection{The types of number}
\label{ssec:number-types}

In Mathilda every value carries a \emph{head} that names its type, and \B{Head} reads
it off. The four numeric heads are \texttt{Integer}, \texttt{Real}, \texttt{Rational},
and \texttt{Complex}:

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{arithmetic/heads/1}{A whole number has head \texttt{Integer}.}
\pair{arithmetic/heads/2}{So does a bignum---an arbitrary-precision integer is still an
\texttt{Integer}, not a separate type.}
\pair{arithmetic/heads/3}{A machine real has head \texttt{Real}.}
\pair{arithmetic/heads/4}{A fraction has head \texttt{Rational}: internally it is
\texttt{Rational[p, q]}.}
\pair{arithmetic/heads/5}{And a complex number has head \texttt{Complex}, stored as
\texttt{Complex[re, im]}.}
\end{codepairs}

These four heads are all Mathilda needs to know a number's kind at a glance. Everything
that follows either combines numbers or takes them apart, so we start with the operators.

\subsection{The arithmetic operators}
\label{ssec:operators}

The operators you type---$+$, $-$, $\times$, $/$, $\wedge$---are surface syntax for a
few built-in heads, and \emph{only a few}. \B{Plus} and \B{Times} and \B{Power} are the
primitives; subtraction, division, and negation are not separate operations but
rewrites into them, as \B{FullForm} reveals:

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{arithmetic/operator-forms/1}{Subtraction is \B{Plus} against a negated term:
$a-b$ is \texttt{Plus[a, Times[-1, b]]}.}
\pair{arithmetic/operator-forms/2}{Division is \B{Times} against a reciprocal power:
$a/b$ is \texttt{Times[a, Power[b, -1]]}.}
\pair{arithmetic/operator-forms/3}{Unary minus is multiplication by $-1$.}
\pair{arithmetic/operator-forms/4}{Even $\sqrt{x}$ is a \B{Power}, with exponent the
exact rational $1/2$.}
\end{codepairs}

Reducing everything to \B{Plus}, \B{Times}, and \B{Power} is what keeps the evaluator
small: a single canonicalizer for each, driven by attributes\calloutnote{Under the
hood}{The attributes do the organising. \texttt{Flat} means nested calls are spliced
(\texttt{Plus[a, Plus[b, c]]} becomes \texttt{Plus[a, b, c]}); \texttt{Orderless} sorts
the arguments into a canonical order so that \texttt{a + b} and \texttt{b + a} are the
same expression and like terms sit together to be combined; \texttt{OneIdentity} lets a
one-argument call collapse; \texttt{Listable} threads the operation over lists; and
\texttt{NumericFunction} marks it for numeric evaluation. Underneath, the exact-integer
paths add and multiply in a $128$-bit intermediate and, on overflow, fall through to
GMP~\cite{gmp}, promoting the result to a bignum---so the answer is always exact, and
the fast machine-word path is taken whenever it fits.}, handles addition,
subtraction, multiplication, division, powers, and roots at once.

\subsection{Machine-precision computation}
\label{ssec:machine-precision}

At the bottom of the tower are the machine numbers: a signed 64-bit integer and an
IEEE-754 double. These are the types the processor implements directly, and Mathilda
uses them whenever it can, because they are fast.

An integer stays a machine word only while it fits in 64 bits. The largest such value
is $2^{63}-1$; one step beyond and Mathilda promotes it to an arbitrary-precision\index{integer overflow!promotion to bignum}
integer, silently and without loss:

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{arithmetic/machine-integers/1}{$2^{62}$ fits in a signed 64-bit word and stays
a machine integer.}
\pair{arithmetic/machine-integers/2}{$2^{63}$ is one past the largest machine integer,
so it is promoted to a GMP bignum---still exact.}
\pair{arithmetic/machine-integers/3}{Addition promotes too: two machine integers whose
sum overflows produce a bignum. The promotion is detected with a wider (128-bit)\index{integer overflow!128-bit intermediate}
intermediate.}
\end{codepairs}

Machine reals are IEEE-754 binary64 (``double'') values. Their characteristics are
reported by a handful of read-only system constants, derived at start-up from the
platform's \texttt{<float.h>}:

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{arithmetic/machine-limits/1}{A double carries about $15.95$ decimal
digits---precisely $53\log_{10}2$, the decimal worth of its 53-bit mantissa\index{mantissa}.}
\pair{arithmetic/machine-limits/2}{\B{\$MachineEpsilon}: the gap between $1$ and the
next representable double.}
\pair{arithmetic/machine-limits/3}{The largest finite double.}
\pair{arithmetic/machine-limits/4}{And the smallest positive normal double.}
\end{codepairs}

Here is the single most important thing to understand about Mathilda's machine reals,
and a real difference from Mathematica: \textbf{Mathilda does not use significance\index{significance arithmetic}
arithmetic}.\calloutnote{Theory}{\emph{Significance arithmetic} (which Mathematica uses
for arbitrary-precision numbers) attaches to each number an estimate of its error and
propagates that estimate through every operation, so that catastrophic cancellation\index{catastrophic cancellation}
visibly reduces the reported precision. It is elegant but only a first-order model of
error, and it makes a number's behaviour depend on its history. Mathilda takes the other
well-trodden road: a machine real is a fixed-precision IEEE double, exactly as specified
by the floating-point standard~\cite{ieee754}. If you need to know how error propagates,
you either reason about it yourself---the classic reference is Goldberg~\cite{goldberg1991}---or
reach for the rigorous tool in \S\ref{ssec:interval}, interval arithmetic.} A machine
real is just an IEEE double, with a fixed 53-bit mantissa; it
carries no error term, and operations do not track how many digits remain trustworthy.
What you get is exactly what the hardware, C, or NumPy would give you---no more and no
less:

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{arithmetic/machine-doubles/1}{Dividing two machine reals gives a machine real,
printed to six figures by default.}
\pair{arithmetic/machine-doubles/2}{$0.1+0.2$ \emph{prints} as \texttt{0.3}, because
six figures round to that.}
\pair{arithmetic/machine-doubles/3}{But it is not exactly $0.3$: the residual is the
true IEEE rounding error\index{rounding error}, shown plainly rather than hidden.}
\pair{arithmetic/machine-doubles/4}{The precision of any machine real is the symbol
\texttt{MachinePrecision}\index{machine precision}.}
\end{codepairs}

This fixed-precision model is not a limitation so much as a deliberate choice, and for
the overwhelming majority of numerical work---linear algebra, differential equations,
optimization, plotting---it is exactly right: it is fast, it is predictable, and it
matches every other serious numerical system bit for bit. When you genuinely need more
than a double can hold, you move up the tower.

\subsection{Arbitrary-precision computation}
\label{ssec:arbitrary-precision}

Exactness in Mathilda is never an option\index{exactness (default)} you switch on; it is the default, and it never
silently degrades. Integers grow without bound, factorials and powers stay exact, and
the arithmetic behind them is GMP~\cite{gmp}, the same library\index{GMP} that underpins much of
computational number theory:

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{arithmetic/bignums/1}{A thirty-one-digit power, kept exactly.}
\pair{arithmetic/bignums/2}{$100!$ is a $158$-digit integer, computed exactly by GMP.}
\pair{arithmetic/bignums/3}{Factoring the Mersenne number $2^{67}-1$\index{Mersenne number}---the number Cole
famously factored by hand in 1903.}
\end{codepairs}

Rational numbers are exact too. A ratio of integers is kept as a reduced fraction\index{number!rational!lowest terms}, its
numerator and denominator sharing no common factor, and it collapses to an integer the
moment its denominator becomes~$1$:

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{arithmetic/rationals/1}{A sum of fractions is another fraction, reduced to lowest
terms.}
\pair{arithmetic/rationals/2}{Reduction happens on construction, so $2/4$ is already
$1/2$.}
\pair{arithmetic/rationals/3}{When the denominator reduces to $1$, the rational
collapses to a plain integer.}
\end{codepairs}

For \emph{approximate} numbers beyond machine precision, Mathilda uses MPFR, which
computes correctly-rounded floating point\index{correctly-rounded arithmetic} to any requested precision. You reach it with
the two-argument form \B{N}\texttt{[expr, d]}, asking for \texttt{d} digits:

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{arithmetic/mpfr-N/1}{$\pi$ to fifty digits, via MPFR's \texttt{mpfr\_const\_pi}.}
\pair{arithmetic/mpfr-N/2}{$e$ to a hundred digits.}
\pair{arithmetic/mpfr-N/3}{$\sqrt2$ to forty digits---an MPFR power, not a table
lookup.}
\pair{arithmetic/mpfr-N/4}{Ap\'ery's constant $\zeta(3)$ to fifty digits. The zeta
function is evaluated by FLINT's rigorous ball arithmetic\index{FLINT}~\cite{flint,arb}, not by
MPFR.}
\end{codepairs}

So the numeric substrate is really three libraries working together: GMP for exact
integers, MPFR for correctly-rounded arbitrary-precision reals, and FLINT/Arb for the
harder special functions, where rigorous complex-ball arithmetic guarantees\index{ball arithmetic} every
printed digit. \B{N} orchestrates them, replacing each exact leaf of an expression with
a numeric value of the requested precision and letting the ordinary evaluator re-run.

A number's precision is itself a value you can query, and here Mathilda is honest about
a small subtlety:

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{arithmetic/precision/1}{Exact numbers have \emph{infinite} precision\index{precision!of exact numbers}.}
\pair{arithmetic/precision/2}{But \B{N}\texttt{[Pi, 50]} reports about $50.27$ digits,
not $50$: fifty decimal digits are stored as $\lceil 50\log_2 10\rceil = 167$ bits\index{precision!decimal digits vs bits},
which is a hair more than fifty digits' worth.}
\pair{arithmetic/precision/3}{You can also state a number's precision on input: the
literal \texttt{1.5`20}\index{precision!input notation (backtick)} is $1.5$ carried at twenty digits.}
\end{codepairs}

Precision is \emph{contagious}\index{numeric contagion}, and when representations of different precision meet, the
lowest wins---machine precision being the floor\index{machine precision!as floor}:

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{arithmetic/contagion/1}{Add a machine real to a hundred-digit number and the
result drops to machine precision: one approximate double poisons the lot.}
\pair{arithmetic/contagion/2}{Bare \B{N} asks for machine precision, so even a
hundred-digit argument comes back as a machine number---to request a specific
precision, use the two-argument \B{N}\texttt{[\emph{expr}, \emph{p}]}.}
\pair{arithmetic/contagion/3}{$1001!$ is about $4\times10^{2570}$, far beyond
\B{\$MaxMachineNumber}. Bare \B{N} still hands it back at machine precision---only the
leading $16$ digits are significant---but does \emph{not} overflow to
\texttt{Infinity}\calloutnote{Under the hood}{A machine number carries a fixed $53$-bit
mantissa but an \emph{arbitrary} exponent. An IEEE double cannot: its exponent tops out
near $10^{308}$. So whenever a machine-precision result would overflow (or flush a
nonzero value to zero), Mathilda keeps a $53$-bit MPFR instead---same mantissa,
unlimited exponent range (\texttt{mpfr\_to\_machine} in \texttt{src/numeric.c}). The
value survives; the precision does not grow.}, so its magnitude survives where a raw
double would not.}
\end{codepairs}

\subsection{Complex numbers}
\label{ssec:complex}

The fourth numeric head, \texttt{Complex}, rounds out the picture. A complex number is
stored as \texttt{Complex[re, im]}\index{number!complex}, and it inherits the same
exact/inexact split as everything else: built from integers it is an exact
\emph{Gaussian integer}\index{Gaussian integer}; built from machine reals it is an
inexact machine complex. Arithmetic on them is automatic---\texttt{Plus},
\texttt{Times}, and \texttt{Power} already know the rules---and a small family of
component functions reads off the parts:

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{arithmetic/complex/1}{Multiplication follows $(a+bi)(c+di)$; the exact integer
parts stay exact, so $(2+3i)(1-i)$ is the Gaussian integer $5+i$.}
\pair{arithmetic/complex/2}{\B{Conjugate} flips the sign of the imaginary part.}
\pair{arithmetic/complex/3}{\B{Abs} is the modulus $\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$; here it is the
integer $5$.}
\pair{arithmetic/complex/4}{But a Gaussian integer's modulus is usually irrational, and
Mathilda keeps it exact: $|1+i|=\sqrt2$, not $1.414\ldots$}
\pair{arithmetic/complex/5}{\B{Arg} is the argument, taken in the range $(-\pi,\pi]$;
for $1+i$ it is $\pi/4$.}
\pair{arithmetic/complex/6}{\B{ReIm} returns the real and imaginary parts together as a
pair (\B{Re} and \B{Im} give them singly).}
\pair{arithmetic/complex/7}{For symbolic work, \B{ComplexExpand} expands on the
assumption that the variables are \emph{real}, so $(a+bi)^2$ becomes $a^2-b^2+2ab\,i$.}
\end{codepairs}

That an exact modulus like $\sqrt2$ is \emph{kept} as $\sqrt2$ is the same principle
that governed the integers and rationals: exactness is the default and does not silently
degrade. Ask for a number with \texttt{N} and you get $1.41421$; leave it and it stays a
radical.

\subsection{Rounding and integer division}
\label{ssec:rounding}

Two everyday tasks turn a real number into an integer: \emph{rounding} it, and
\emph{dividing} it with a remainder. Mathilda spells them with the functions you would
expect, and---because exactness is the default---an exact input returns an exact integer.

\B{Floor} and \B{Ceiling} round down and up; \B{Round} rounds to the nearest integer;
\B{IntegerPart} and \B{FractionalPart} split off the integer and fractional pieces:

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{arithmetic/rounding/1}{\B{Floor} rounds toward $-\infty$: $\lfloor 7/2\rfloor=3$.}
\pair{arithmetic/rounding/2}{\B{Ceiling} rounds toward $+\infty$.}
\pair{arithmetic/rounding/3}{\B{Round} goes to the nearest integer, breaking a tie
toward the nearest \emph{even} one\index{rounding!banker's}---so $5/2$ rounds to $2$.}
\pair{arithmetic/rounding/4}{The same rule sends $7/2$ to $4$, not $3$. This is
\emph{banker's rounding}, which avoids the upward bias of always rounding halves up.}
\pair{arithmetic/rounding/5}{It applies to machine reals too: $2.5$ rounds to $2$.}
\pair{arithmetic/rounding/6}{\B{IntegerPart} truncates \emph{toward zero}, so
$\mathrm{IntegerPart}(-3.7)=-3$---where \B{Floor} would give $-4$.}
\pair{arithmetic/rounding/7}{\B{FractionalPart} is the remainder, carrying the sign of
the argument ($-0.7$), so that the two pieces add back to $-3.7$.}
\pair{arithmetic/rounding/8}{Each takes an optional second argument, a unit to round to:
\B{Floor}\texttt{[7.3, 0.5]} is the largest multiple of $0.5$ not exceeding $7.3$.}
\end{codepairs}

Integer division is the companion pair \B{Quotient} and \B{Mod}, with
\B{QuotientRemainder} giving both at once. Mathilda uses \emph{floored}
division\index{division!floored}\calloutnote{Pitfall}{Many languages---C, and much that
follows it---use \emph{truncated} division instead: the quotient rounds toward zero and
the remainder takes the sign of the \emph{dividend}, so C's \texttt{-17 \% 5} is $-2$.
Mathilda's \texttt{Mod[-17, 5]} is $3$. Neither is wrong; they make opposite choices for
the sign of the remainder. The floored convention is the number-theorist's, and the one
that keeps \texttt{Mod[m, n]} inside $[0, n)$ for positive $n$.}: the quotient rounds
toward $-\infty$, and the remainder takes the sign of the \emph{divisor}.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{arithmetic/division/1}{\B{Quotient} is the integer part of $17/5$.}
\pair{arithmetic/division/2}{\B{Mod} is the remainder that is left, $2$.}
\pair{arithmetic/division/3}{With a negative dividend the quotient floors to $-4$\ldots}
\pair{arithmetic/division/4}{\ldots and the remainder is $3$, not $-2$: it carries the
sign of the divisor. The two always satisfy $n\,\mathrm{Quotient}[m,n]+\mathrm{Mod}[m,n]=m$.}
\pair{arithmetic/division/5}{\B{QuotientRemainder} returns the pair
$\{\text{quotient},\ \text{remainder}\}$ in a single call.}
\pair{arithmetic/division/6}{Two integers' greatest common divisor is \B{GCD}\ldots}
\pair{arithmetic/division/7}{\ldots and their least common multiple is \B{LCM}. Number
theory proper---primes, factorization, modular powers---is the subject of
\S\ref{sec:numbertheory}.}
\end{codepairs}

\subsection{Rational parts and cleanup}
\label{ssec:parts}

Finally, three functions take a number apart or tidy it up. \B{Numerator} and
\B{Denominator} split a rational; \B{Rationalize} runs the other way, finding a rational
close to an approximate number; and \B{Chop} discards the tiny residues that
floating-point arithmetic leaves behind.

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{arithmetic/parts/1}{\B{Numerator} of $6/8$---already reduced to $3/4$ on
construction---is $3$.}
\pair{arithmetic/parts/2}{\B{Denominator} is $4$.}
\pair{arithmetic/parts/3}{\B{Rationalize} recovers the exact fraction behind a machine
real: $0.75$ is $3/4$.}
\pair{arithmetic/parts/4}{With a tolerance it finds the \emph{simplest} rational within
that distance---famously, $\pi$ to two decimals is $22/7$.}
\pair{arithmetic/parts/5}{And \B{Chop} rounds a near-zero machine value to an exact $0$:
the $0.1+0.2-0.3$ residue from \S\ref{ssec:machine-precision} disappears.}
\end{codepairs}

\subsection{Interval arithmetic}
\label{ssec:interval}

The tools so far give you either an exact answer or an approximate one whose error you
must reason about yourself. \emph{Interval arithmetic} gives you a third thing\index{interval arithmetic}: an
approximate answer that comes with a \emph{proof}. Instead of a single number, you
compute with an interval $[a,b]$ that is guaranteed to contain the true value, and every
operation returns an interval guaranteed to contain the true result. The idea goes back
to Moore~\cite{moore2009}\index{Moore, R. E.}; Mathilda implements it as a first-class numeric object,
\B{Interval}\texttt{[\{lo, hi\}]}.\calloutnote{Pitfall}{Two of Mathilda's interval
conventions are worth stating up front. A machine number becomes a \emph{point}
interval---\texttt{Interval[1.]}\index{interval arithmetic!point interval} is
\texttt{Interval[\{1., 1.\}]}---rather than being preloaded with a half-ULP uncertainty
band; if you want an uncertainty propagated, you supply it. And \B{Interval} is a set,
not a scalar, so it is deliberately neither \texttt{Listable} nor a
\texttt{NumericFunction}.}

The arithmetic operations and the elementary functions all propagate intervals:

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{arithmetic/interval-basic/1}{Interval addition: the sum of $[1,2]$ and $[3,4]$ is
$[4,6]$.}
\pair{arithmetic/interval-basic/2}{Multiplication takes the extremes over the four
corner products.}
\pair{arithmetic/interval-basic/3}{Squaring is tighter than multiplying an interval by
itself: $[-2,5]^2=[0,25]$, because the operation knows the two factors are equal.}
\pair{arithmetic/interval-basic/4}{A reciprocal that straddles zero splits into a
disjoint union of two rays\index{interval arithmetic!disjoint union}---an interval may be several ranges at once.}
\pair{arithmetic/interval-basic/5}{Functions thread too: $\sqrt{[4,9]}=[2,3]$.}
\end{codepairs}

What makes these \emph{rigorous} rather than merely suggestive is \emph{outward\index{interval arithmetic!outward rounding}
rounding}. Exact endpoints (integers, rationals, $\pi$) pass through untouched, but the
moment an endpoint becomes an inexact machine or MPFR real, Mathilda nudges it one unit\index{ulp (unit in the last place)}
in the last place \emph{outward}---lower bounds down, upper bounds up---so the printed
interval is a mathematically certain enclosure, never an underestimate. There is no
dependence on an external interval library; the widening is done directly with
\texttt{nextafter} and MPFR's directed neighbours.

The classic application is certifying a floating-point computation. Recall that
$0.1+0.2-0.3$ was not exactly zero; interval arithmetic pins down exactly where the true
answer lies:

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{arithmetic/interval-rounding/1}{A certified enclosure of the rounding error: the
true value of $0.1+0.2-0.3$ lies somewhere in this tiny interval around zero.}
\pair{arithmetic/interval-rounding/2}{And zero is provably inside it---so the exact
answer really is $0$, rounding error notwithstanding.}
\end{codepairs}

A more striking use is proving that a solution \emph{exists}. If the image of a
continuous function over an interval straddles zero, the function must have a root there.
Take a razor-thin bracket of $\pi$---the classic approximation $355/113$ as the upper
endpoint, both endpoints now inexact machine reals---and evaluate $\sin$ across it:

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{arithmetic/interval-root/1}{The enclosure of $\sin$ over $[245/78,\,355/113]$ runs
from a small negative value to a small positive one. The endpoints are inexact, but
outward rounding keeps the enclosure a rigorous over-estimate.}
\pair{arithmetic/interval-root/2}{Zero lies inside it---even the inexact $0.$. This is a
computer-assisted proof that $\sin$ has a root\index{computer-assisted proof} in $[245/78, 355/113]$.}
\end{codepairs}

Interval arithmetic has one famous limitation, the \emph{dependency problem}\index{interval arithmetic!dependency problem}: because
each occurrence of a variable is treated independently, an expression like $x-x$ is not
recognised as zero, and the enclosure it returns is wider than the true range. This is
not a bug but a property of the method, and it means naive interval evaluation can
over-estimate:

\begin{codepairs}
\pair{arithmetic/interval-advanced/1}{$x-x$ over $[-1,1]$ gives $[-2,2]$, not $\{0\}$:
the two copies are treated as independent.}
\pair{arithmetic/interval-advanced/2}{Iterating the logistic map $4x(1-x)$\index{logistic map} on a point
interval, the enclosure widens at each step---a certified picture of how rounding error
grows in a chaotic system, always containing the true orbit.}
\end{codepairs}

Interval arithmetic has been standardized: IEEE~1788--2015\index{interval arithmetic!IEEE 1788}~\cite{ieee1788}
fixes what the operations mean and how a conforming implementation must behave. Mathilda
follows its \emph{set-based} model---an interval denotes the set of reals it contains,
and every operation returns an enclosure of the true result set---which is the flavor
used throughout this section. Two parts of the standard Mathilda deliberately does
\emph{not} implement. The first is the \emph{decoration}\index{interval arithmetic!decoration}
system\calloutnote{Theory}{A \emph{decoration} is a tag the standard attaches to an
interval, recording what happened as it was computed: whether the computation stayed
inside every function's domain and was continuous there (\texttt{com}, \texttt{dac}),
whether a domain violation occurred (\texttt{trv}), or whether it was ill-formed
(\texttt{ill}). Carried alongside the interval, decorations let a long chain of
operations certify \emph{at the end} that nothing stepped outside a function's domain
along the way. Mathilda tracks the enclosure but not this provenance tag.}: its
enclosures are rigorous, but they do not carry a tag certifying that every intermediate
step stayed within its function's domain. The second is the standard's alternative
\emph{flavors}, such as the modal (Kaucher) intervals. What Mathilda does
guarantee---outward-rounded, mathematically certain enclosures, including the disjoint
unions that arise from dividing by a straddling interval---is precisely the common core
those extensions are built on.

That completes the tour of Mathilda's numbers. We have climbed the tower from machine
integers through GMP bignums, exact rationals, and machine and MPFR reals to complex
numbers; watched precision flow and seen where it stops; met the everyday functions that
round, divide, and take a number apart; and finished with intervals, which trade a
single value for a guaranteed enclosure. The algebra of \S\ref{sec:algebra} builds
directly on the exact arithmetic established here, and the number-theoretic functions
glimpsed above---\texttt{GCD}, factorization, modular powers---are taken up in earnest in
\S\ref{sec:numbertheory}. For the exhaustive options and corner cases of any function
named in this section, its reference page is one \texttt{?Name} away at the prompt.
